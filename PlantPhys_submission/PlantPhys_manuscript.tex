\abstract{Grain nitrogen (N) in maize depends on N uptake, assimilation, and allocation, but it is unclear whether the same metabolic functions underlie natural variation in grain amino acid composition, genetic variation associated with soil N, and response to selection. We tested this in three populations, each with a distinct N-related phenotype. In the Goodman--Buckler Association Panel, grain free amino acids were dominated by asparagine and proline and were highly heritable, and genome-wide association mapping of 71 traits implicated genes for amino acid metabolism and transport. The loci shared across the most traits lay near a peptidase A1 gene and endoplasmic reticulum membrane protein genes. In 3,107 georeferenced landraces from across the Americas, association mapping of soil N at collection sites identified variants near an aspartate kinase isozyme and an omega-amidase that passed tests for confounding with geography and climate. Indian Chief and Jarvis responded to 14 cycles of reciprocal recurrent selection for yield with different changes in N allocation. The two populations diverged genetically, and Indian Chief accumulated more free amino acids, mostly proline, while protein-bound amino acids were unchanged. Their candidate regions included protein-turnover genes in Indian Chief and amino acid importers in Jarvis, consistent with these population-specific phenotypes. Each of the four analyses highlighted a different enzyme of the aspartate-to-homoserine pathway, the only pathway that remained significant after correction across all pathways. Together, these results point to aspartate-family metabolism as a shared source of candidate genes for how N is partitioned among grain amino acid pools in maize.}

\keywords{maize, grain nitrogen, free amino acids, aspartate-family amino acids, genome-wide association, reciprocal recurrent selection}

\section{Introduction}

Nitrogen (N) is essential for maize growth and kernel development, but its use in agriculture is a major sustainability challenge. Crop production relies heavily on N fertilizer, yet much of the applied N is not recovered by the crop and is lost through leaching, runoff, or gaseous emissions \citep{OjedaRivera2025NitrogenEfficientMaize}. Reducing these losses requires understanding how plants acquire, assimilate, transport, and remobilize N, and how they allocate it to the developing grain \citep{MasclauxDaubresse2010NUE,Have2017NRemobilization}. In maize kernels, N is held in free amino acids (FAAs) and protein-bound amino acids (PBAAs), so grain amino acid composition is a direct biochemical readout of how N is allocated to the grain.

Plant N metabolism centers on a few key amino acids. Ammonium is assimilated via the glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase (GS/GOGAT) cycle, producing glutamine and regenerating glutamate from 2-oxoglutarate, so Glu/Gln metabolism links N assimilation with carbon metabolism \citep{MasclauxDaubresse2010NUE,Hodges2002TwoOxoglutarate}. Glutamine and asparagine are the main N-rich transport and storage forms, while glutamate and aspartate donate amino groups to carbon skeletons \citep{Bernard2009CytosolicGS,MasclauxDaubresse2010NUE}. Aspartate is particularly important because it feeds asparagine and the aspartate-family amino acids (methionine, threonine, lysine, isoleucine) \citep{Viola2001AspartatePathway, Wang2018AspSeeds, Han2021Aspartate}. During grain filling, protein degradation in senescing leaves releases amino acids that supply grain N \citep{Have2017NRemobilization, Liu2008ProteinDegradationNRemobilization}. Proline adds a stress-related component, with genotype-dependent accumulation and export from maize leaves under water stress \citep{Carceller1999ProlineRemobilization}. Thus, shifts in grain FAA composition may reflect redistribution among multiple N pools, not just changes in total N.

Most grain N is stored in storage proteins, so protein-bound amino acid composition is largely determined by the proteins produced. The free amino acid pool is small but reflects amino acids delivered to and metabolized in the developing kernel, and may therefore better indicate N supply. How much this pool varies heritably among lines, and which genes control that variation, is less well known than for protein-bound amino acids \citep{Shrestha2022}.

Maize germplasm enables grain N metabolism to be studied in three ways. First, standing variation among inbred lines. The Goodman--Buckler panel captures much of the allelic diversity in public breeding and has been used to map grain composition traits \citep{FlintGarcia2005Goodman,Shrestha2022}. Second, adaptation to soil. The landraces evolved under local conditions across the Americas, so alleles affecting N acquisition or use may correlate with soil N at collection sites. Georeferenced landraces enable environmental GWAS \citep{RomeroNavarro2017Landraces}, but soil N covaries with geography and climate and must be controlled. Third, selection for yield. Fourteen cycles of reciprocal recurrent selection in Indian Chief and Jarvis altered N uptake and allocation \citep{Comstock1949RRS,MollStuber1971}. Jarvis increased grain N via post-anthesis uptake, while Indian Chief retained more N in stover \citep{Moll1994}. Comparing Cycle 0 vs. Cycle 14 highlights regions with large allele-frequency shifts, though drift is also substantial in small populations \citep{Gerke2015DriftRRS}.

These populations differ in germplasm, in the N-related phenotype measured, and in how genetic signals are detected, so the same genes are not expected to appear in all of them. Each analysis also yields hundreds to thousands of candidate genes, many of which overlap by chance. If N metabolism shapes all three, however, different genes in the same metabolic pathways could be implicated. We therefore compared the analyses at the pathway level, asking whether any pathway contained candidates from more analyses than expected by chance.

We combined grain amino acid profiling and GWAS in the Goodman--Buckler panel, soil-N environmental GWAS in landraces, genomic population scans of Indian Chief and Jarvis, and reanalyzed the B64 $\times$ CML14 biparental population developed for grain asparagine variation for aspartate and proline. We analyzed free and protein-bound amino acid pools, focusing on asparagine, aspartate, glutamate, glutamine, and proline because of their roles in N transport, C--N metabolism, and storage. Although candidate regions overlapped no more than expected by chance, each analysis implicated a different enzyme of the aspartate-to-homoserine pathway, the only pathway that remained significant after correction. Beyond this pathway, individual candidates pointed to the aspartate--glutamate junction, where amino acid N connects with C--N recycling, and to amino acid transport and protein turnover, with different functions highlighted in each population. Together, these results suggest that aspartate-family metabolism is a shared source of variation in maize N metabolism.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Kernel free amino acid composition in the Goodman--Buckler panel is dominated by asparagine and proline}

FAA composition was evaluated across 280 of the 282 lines in the Goodman--Buckler panel grown in four replicates (two years $\times$ two blocks, Supplemental Tables S1--S2). Asn and Pro were the most abundant amino acids. Asn ranked first in 146 genotypes (52\%) and Pro in 118 (42\%), and the two were the top amino acids in 179 of 280 genotypes (64\%, Supplemental Figure~S1A--B). Asn was about 144 times as abundant as Cys (Supplemental Figure~S1C), but abundance did not predict variability among lines. Met varied the most (coefficient of variation, CV, 180\%), while Glu, Trp, and Gly varied the least (CV 44--54\%, Supplemental Table S3).

Differences among lines were consistent across years. For the 251 lines grown in both years, line means were correlated between years for all free amino acids (Spearman $\rho = 0.33$--0.75, median 0.56, Supplemental Table S3). Median broad-sense heritability ($H^2$) was 0.75 for the 20 FAAs (0.47--0.88), compared with 0.60 for the 15 PBAAs measured by \citet{Shrestha2022}, and was highest for Lys (0.88), Asn (0.83), Asp (0.81), and Pro (0.80). Median genomic heritability ($h^2$) was 0.48 for FAAs and 0.34 for PBAAs (Supplemental Figure~S2, Supplemental Table S4).

The protein-bound pool dominated grain amino acids, exceeding the free pool 47-fold for total amino acids and 42-fold for amino acid-derived N, in which the amount of each amino acid weighted by its number of N atoms (paired Wilcoxon tests, all $P < 10^{-40}$, Supplemental Figure~S3, Supplemental Table S5). So, FAAs contributed only 2.4\% of grain amino acid-derived N. However, composition differed between the pools. Pro made up 23.1\% of FAAs but 10.6\% of PBAAs, and its fraction varied far more among lines in the free pool (CV 44\% compared with 8\%). Thus, the free pool is small but variable and reproducible among lines, which makes it suitable for association mapping.

\subsection{Association mapping identifies amino acid metabolism and transport candidates for abundant, heritable free amino acids}

We ran GWAS for 78 amino acid phenotypes (Supplemental Table S2) with three models (MLM, BLINK, and FarmCPU). At a Benjamini--Hochberg FDR of 5\%, 2,461 SNPs were significant for at least one of 71 phenotypes. Significant SNPs within 25 kb of each other identified 3,693 candidate genes (Supplemental Tables S6--S7). Most candidate genes were specific to one phenotype (3,002), and only 193 were shared by more than two. A locus on chromosome 9 (133.93 Mb) associated with nine phenotypes contained an ER membrane protein complex subunit 4 gene (\textit{Zm00001eb394360}) and a rhomboid-like protein gene (\textit{Zm00001eb394380}) (Supplemental Figure S4). Similarly, a locus on chromosome 4 near a peptidase A1 (aspartic protease) gene (\textit{Zm00001eb190800} at 174.57 Mb) was associated with eight phenotypes spanning four biosynthetic families (\textit{A}, \textit{V}, \textit{K}, \textit{F}, \textit{T}, \textit{LAV}, \textit{IVL}, and \textit{E.Total}) (Figure~\ref{fig:amino_locus_manhattan}).

Among individual FAAs (Table~\ref{tab:amino_gwas_focus}), Asn had the most loci (356 across abundance and five ratios). Candidates included homoserine dehydrogenase (\textit{Zm00001eb405410}), asparagine synthetase domain and Lys-branch genes, and the vacuolar transporter AVT6C (\textit{Zm00001eb368680}). A SNP for Lys fraction mapped to the NRT1/PTR family 4.6 transporter \textit{Zm00001eb277160}, and Gln SNPs mapped to a WAT1-related (UmamiT) gene and the amino acid permease \textit{aaap40}. Pro had fewer loci (59) and lower heritability ($h^2 = 0.40$ vs. 0.68), and Met, the most variable FAA, lacked a trait-matched candidate. Overall, Asn/Asp-family metabolism and amino acid transport genes were linked to specific amino acids, whereas loci shared across many phenotypes lay near genes for protein turnover and membrane proteins rather than biosynthetic enzymes.

To test whether free Asp and Pro variation also segregates in a biparental cross, we mapped both traits in a B64 $\times$ CML14 F$_{2:3}$ population developed to map kernel free Asn \citep{Fitzsimmons2026KernelKitchen}. Because the parents were chosen for Asn, this analysis was exploratory (Supplemental Figure S5A,C). Both traits mapped to the same position on chromosome 3 (peak at 35.99~Mb), with LOD 6.12 for Asp (threshold 3.70) and 4.55 for Pro (threshold 3.63), explaining 10\% and 7\% of phenotypic variance (Supplemental Figure S5B,D). This shared QTL was distinct from the Asn QTL at \textit{ASN3} on chromosome~1. Its support interval spans the low-recombination region around the centromere and contains more than 1,500 genes (Supplemental Table S8), so it cannot resolve a causal gene. Within 1.5~cM of the peak, it does, however, include the tandem CC-type glutaredoxin genes (\textit{Zm00001eb131830}, \textit{Zm00001eb131840}) and an LHT-like amino acid transporter (\textit{Zm00001eb132750}), all of which also lie in highly differentiated Jarvis windows explained below.

\subsection{Soil nitrogen environment GWAS of maize landraces points to candidate genes in aspartate-family and amide nitrogen metabolism}

In the Goodman--Buckler panel, the grain free amino acid pool was dominated by Asn and Pro, which, along with Lys and Asp, were also among the most heritable free amino acids, and association mapping identified candidate genes in Asn and Asp-family metabolism and amino acid transport. Because amino acids are the main transport form of N in the plant \citep{MasclauxDaubresse2010NUE}, their metabolism may also respond to N availability in the soil. However, these inbred lines represent standing variation in temperate germplasm grown under managed fertility. We therefore turned to maize landraces collected across a soil N gradient (Figure~\ref{fig:soilN_gwas}B) and asked whether genetic variation associated with soil N implicates similar N-metabolism functions. Because soil N describes the collection site rather than a grain phenotype, we did not equate associations with soil N with effects on grain amino acid composition. We associated 3,107 georeferenced landraces \citep{RomeroNavarro2017Landraces} with soil N at their collection sites \citep{Poggio2021SoilGrids}, using 0--5 cm as the primary phenotype and 5--15 cm for validation ($r = 0.84$, Figure~\ref{fig:soilN_gwas}A--B). Because accessions from the same or nearby sites share both soil N values and ancestry, genomic heritability was $\sim$1.00 and remained 0.98 even after removing 50 genotype principal components (permuted $h^2 \le 0.05$, Figure~\ref{fig:soilN_gwas}C), reflecting shared ancestry and location rather than heritable variation in the plants. Across the three GWAS models, 234 SNPs were significant at a 5\% FDR, with 15 of them in at least two models (Figure~\ref{fig:soilN_gwas}D), and 448 genes identified within a 25 kb region (Supplemental Table S9).

Since landrace ancestry mirrors geographic patterns, alleles that are prevalent within a region might be incorrectly interpreted as being associated with soil N.We conducted two tests to determine whether the associations were driven by soil N rather than by geography or climate. First, we repeated the GWAS for geography (latitude, longitude, and elevation) and soil properties (soil N 5--15 cm, soil pH, CEC, and aridity), and compared their significant SNPs with the soil N SNPs (Supplemental Figure~S6, Supplemental Tables S10--S11). Geographic variables overlapped strongly, with 18.2\% of latitude SNPs and 15.0\% of longitude SNPs being significant for another geographic variable, whereas only 2 of 234 soil N SNPs (0.9\%) overlapped (Supplemental Figure S6A), similar to the 0.7 expected by chance ($P = 0.14$). Soil N, however, overlapped more than expected with SNPs for the 5--15 cm layer (51), aridity (38 vs 0.2 expected), and soil pH (13 vs 0.1), consistent with covariation with soil N (Supplemental Figure S6B).

Second, SNPs may track geographic gradients yet be nonsignificant in GWASs. We correlated each SNP genotype with site variables across 3,107 accessions and called a SNP "geography-linked" if it ranked in the top 1\% genome-wide for latitude, longitude, or elevation (Supplemental Figure S7, Supplemental Tables S12--S13). By this criterion, 1.8\% of all 495,755 SNPs in the genome were geography-linked. For comparison, we took the 4,957 SNPs whose genotypes correlated most strongly with soil N (the top 1\%), selected by correlation alone (without a GWAS model), and 35.9\% of these were geography-linked. Among the 234 soil N GWAS SNPs, 13.2\% were geography-linked (Supplemental Figure~S7B, binomial tests, $P = 1.1 \times 10^{-17}$ against all SNPs and $P = 5.9 \times 10^{-15}$ against the 4,957 soil N-correlated SNPs). Thus, GWAS reduced but did not eliminate the geographic signal. Overall, 168/234 SNPs passed both tests. We used these ranks to sort each SNP into one of three groups (Supplemental Figure~S7A). A SNP was considered linked to geography or climate if its genotype was in the top 1\% genome-wide for correlation with latitude, longitude, elevation, soil pH, CEC, or aridity. A SNP was considered not linked if it was not in the top 1\% for any of these variables but was in the top 5\% for correlation with soil N. All other SNPs were labeled inconclusive because their soil N correlations were weak before population-structure correction, preventing comparison with the other variables. Of the 15 SNPs detected by $\geq$2 models (Supplemental Figure S7A), three were linked to geography/climate, including the strongest signal (S9\_151742636, $P = 2.6 \times 10^{-13}$), which was also associated with aridity and soil pH. Five were not linked, and seven were inconclusive (Table~\ref{tab:soilN_specific}).

Two of these 12 SNPs lie near genes of Asp-family or amide N (Asn and Gln) metabolism. S5\_22338979, the only soil N SNP significant in all three models and also significant at 5--15~cm, lies 4.4~kb upstream of \textit{Zm00001eb220040}, an amino acid kinase-domain gene annotated with aspartate kinase activity, the first step of the Asp-derived amino acid pathway. S1\_16128632 lies within \textit{Zm00001eb005710}, an $\omega$-amidase/NIT2-like CN-hydrolase that releases ammonium from the breakdown products of Gln and Asn \citep{Zhang2014OmegaAmidase}. Both SNPs are rare (minor allele frequencies of 0.030 and 0.016), but the minor allele carriers came from 115 and 56 collection sites in 11 countries, so neither association rests on a single site (Supplemental Figure~S8A, C). Carriers came from soils with more N than non-carriers (median 356 compared with 248 for aspartate kinase and 414 compared with 251 for $\omega$-amidase, Kruskal--Wallis $P = 3.3 \times 10^{-10}$ and $4.4 \times 10^{-9}$; Supplemental Figure~S8B, D). Because carriers were concentrated in Mexico and Central America, we also compared them within Mexico, where carriers of both alleles still came from N-richer soils (median 268 compared with 232 for aspartate kinase, $P = 6.4 \times 10^{-4}$, and 424 compared with 233 for $\omega$-amidase, $P = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$). Thus, the soil N GWAS, like the grain amino acid GWAS, points to Asp-family and amide N metabolism, although shared ancestry cannot be fully excluded.

\subsection{Free amino acid profiles of Indian Chief and Jarvis show population-specific remodeling of the soluble amino acid pool}

The Goodman--Buckler panel captured standing variation in grain amino acids, and the landraces captured variation tied to soil N. Both pointed to genes for N and amino acid metabolism, including an aspartate kinase isozyme in the landraces, but neither shows how the grain amino acid pool responds to selection for yield. Indian Chief and Jarvis let us ask whether this pool, and Asp in particular, changes when selection alters N allocation in different ways. Both were base populations in 14 cycles of reciprocal recurrent selection for yield, and selection shifted N allocation differently in the two \citep{Moll1994}. Under nonlimiting N, Jarvis Cycle~14 increased grain N mainly through greater grain weight, driven largely by higher post-anthesis N uptake, with little change in apparent stover-to-grain N remobilization. Indian Chief Cycle~14 kept more stover biomass after anthesis, lost less stover N, and did not significantly increase grain N \citep{Moll1994}. Thus, gains in grain N can come from better recovery of soil N or from changes in how N is allocated within the plant during grain filling \citep{Mueller2019NUE}. We treat these profiles as evidence of changes in the grain amino acid pool, not of changes in N allocation or remobilization. Within each population, we compared total FAA, the fractions of individual amino acids, and FAA-derived N (Figure~\ref{fig:ic_j_faa}, Supplemental Table~S14).

Indian Chief changed in both total FAA and composition. Total FAA per mg increased from 1.40 in Cycle~0 to 2.46 in Cycle~14 (Wilcoxon $P = 0.0043$, BH $P = 0.017$, Figure~\ref{fig:ic_j_faa}A), with no overlap between cycles. Pro increased from 0.320 to 0.519 of total FAA (BH $P = 0.025$), whereas Asp decreased from 0.115 to 0.075 (BH $P = 0.025$) and Glu from 0.140 to 0.062 (BH $P = 0.018$), while Asn and Gln remained unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:ic_j_faa}B). In Jarvis, which had only three Cycle~0 samples, the Asp fraction increased nominally from 0.075 to 0.137 ($P = 0.019$, BH $P = 0.15$), and no other measures changed (Supplemental Table S14).

Protein-bound composition did not change in either population (Supplemental Figure~S9, Supplemental Tables~S15--S16). In Indian Chief, FAA-derived N increased from 1.75 to 2.99 per mg, but PBAA-derived N was more than 100-fold larger and unchanged, as was total amino acid-derived N ($P = 0.42$, Supplemental Figure~S10, Supplemental Table~S17). Thus, after 14 cycles of selection, Indian Chief had more free amino acids and a composition shifted from Asp and Glu toward Pro, whereas Jarvis showed no corrected change, and neither population changed in the protein-bound pool that holds most grain amino acid N.

\subsection{Skim sequencing reveals that Indian Chief and Jarvis diverged during reciprocal recurrent selection}

We skim sequenced 379 individuals from Indian Chief and Jarvis at Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 (mean depth 0.73$\times$, Supplemental Figure~S11). Because the depth was higher at Cycle~14 than at Cycle~0, hard-called genotypes would differ between cycles for technical reasons, so we used genotype likelihoods throughout (Supplemental Figure~S12). Ancestry estimation separated the four population--cycle groups, with runs converging at $K = 4$, where each group formed a distinct cluster (Figure~\ref{fig:ic_j_structure}A). At $K = 2$--3, Indian Chief Cycle~0 was inferred as a mixture of the Jarvis Cycle~0 and Indian Chief Cycle~14 clusters. In the PCA, PC1 (14.3\%) separated Indian Chief Cycle~14 from Jarvis Cycle~14, with both Cycle~0 groups near the center, and PC2 (6.7\%) separated Cycle~14 from Cycle~0 (Figure~\ref{fig:ic_j_structure}B).

$F_{ST}$ between Indian Chief and Jarvis nearly doubled, from 0.111 at Cycle~0 to 0.211 at Cycle~14, on all ten chromosomes (Figure~\ref{fig:ic_j_structure}C). Because Cycle~14 was sequenced more deeply, which should reduce rather than inflate $F_{ST}$, the increase is unlikely to be a depth artifact. This divergence is expected under reciprocal recurrent selection, which favors complementary alleles \citep{Comstock1949RRS}, and is reinforced by drift in small, separately maintained populations.

\subsection{$F_{ST}$ scans identify population-specific candidate regions in Indian Chief and Jarvis}

Before scanning for regions of high differentiation, we asked how much allele-frequency change drift could explain. Each cycle was formed from the selfed progenies of the 20 best plants per population \citep{MollStuber1971}, implying an effective population size ($N_e$) of at most 20. Genome-wide mean $F_{ST}$ between Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 was 0.095 in Indian Chief and 0.114 in Jarvis (Figure~\ref{fig:ic_j_fst}), values reproduced by neutral simulations at $N_e \approx 30$ and 26 (Supplemental Figure~S13). Most genome-wide change is therefore consistent with drift, as found for other maize populations under reciprocal recurrent selection \citep{Gerke2015DriftRRS}, and at this $N_e$ individual outlier regions cannot be formally distinguished from drift. We therefore treat the scans as exploratory and comparative to other analyses in this study.

We retained the top 5\% of $F_{ST}$ windows in each population (109 of 2,162 in Indian Chief and 108 of 2,151 in Jarvis), which contained 2,001 and 1,179 genes (Figure~\ref{fig:ic_j_fst}, Supplemental Tables~S18--S21). Only five regions (2.5~Mb, 73 genes) were in the top 5\% in both populations, no more than expected by chance ($P = 0.80$). We also asked whether the 2,152 amino acid GWAS loci from the Goodman--Buckler panel fell in these windows more often than expected. Top windows in either population covered 8.9\% of the genome and contained 192 loci (8.9\%), no more than expected when the windows were shifted to random positions 10,000 times ($P = 0.52$). Thus, the $F_{ST}$ candidate regions did not overlap the amino acid GWAS loci more than expected by chance.

Because each candidate set contains more than 1,000 genes, we focused on genes in the functions highlighted by the GWASs (Asp-family and Glu/Gln metabolism, amino acid and N transport, and protein turnover) and treated them as candidates of interest rather than evidence of selection. Indian Chief regions included aspartate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase (\textit{Zm00001eb214360}), threonine synthase (\textit{Zm00001eb294790}), two glutamate dehydrogenases (\textit{Zm00001eb060520}, \textit{Zm00001eb162300}), a glutamine synthetase (\textit{Zm00001eb162900}), the glutamine amidotransferase also associated with free Pro in the GWAS (\textit{Zm00001eb217220}), the senescence protease \textit{SAG39} (\textit{Zm00001eb214780}), and autophagy-related genes. Jarvis regions included aspartate kinase (\textit{Zm00001eb094670}), an aspartate aminotransferase (\textit{Zm00001eb152450}, GOT1/THP3), LHT-like (\textit{Zm00001eb132750}) and ProT-like (\textit{Zm00001eb159580}) transporters, NPL4-like protein 1 (\textit{Zm00001eb146560}), and two CC-type glutaredoxins (\textit{Zm00001eb131830}, \textit{Zm00001eb131840}). These candidates require functional validation.

\subsection{The aspartate-to-homoserine pathway recurs across the three populations}

Because the three populations differed in germplasm, phenotypes, and methods, we did not expect the four candidate sets (amino acid GWAS, soil N GWAS, and the Indian Chief and Jarvis $F_{ST}$ scans) to share the same genes. However, if N metabolism affects all three populations, different genes in the same pathways could be involved. We therefore tested whether any of the 468 CornCyc pathways \citep{Hawkins2021PMN} contained candidate genes from more than one of the four sets beyond what would be expected by chance, using a null distribution in which each set’s intervals were randomly shifted along the chromosomes. To limit spurious pathway hits from large candidate sets, we used genes within 25 kb of Bonferroni-significant SNPs from both GWAS and the top 3% of $F_{ST}$ windows.

L-homoserine biosynthesis had candidates in all four analyses and the lowest empirical permutation $P$ of 468 pathways ($P=0.00034$). It was the only pathway significant after multiple-testing correction (corrected $P=0.021$, Figure~\ref{fig:pathway_recurrence}). Each analysis implicated a different enzyme: homoserine dehydrogenase (\textit{Zm00001eb405410}; amino acid GWAS, Asn relative to Asp-family amino acids, \textit{N.IMNTDK}), aspartate kinase isozyme (\textit{Zm00001eb220040}; soil N GWAS), aspartate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase (\textit{Zm00001eb214360}; Indian Chief), and aspartate kinase (\textit{Zm00001eb094670}; Jarvis). L-lysine biosynthesis VI also had candidates from all four analyses ($P=0.0030$), likely because it shares the aspartate kinase and aspartate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase steps. Other pathways with $P<0.05$ were small terpenoid and vitamin E pathways supported by two or three analyses, and none remained significant after correction.

Furthermore, we repeated the test across 12 combinations of GWAS thresholds (FDR 5\%, 3\%, 1\%, Bonferroni) and $F_{ST}$ window thresholds (top 5\%, 3\%, 1\%; Supplemental Figure~S14). Using the top 5\% or 3\% of windows, the same four genes were recovered at all GWAS thresholds, and L-homoserine biosynthesis ranked 1st or 2nd among 468 pathways in all eight combinations. Only with the top 1\% of windows did ranks drop, because Indian Chief and Jarvis were excluded (Supplemental Figure~S14B). With the largest candidate sets (FDR 5\% and top 5\% windows), more pathways were hit by chance and the pathway was not significant after correction (corrected $P = 0.68$). Overall, L-homoserine biosynthesis ranked consistently, but corrected significance depended on candidate-set size.

\section{Discussion}

We tested whether natural variation in grain amino acids, genetic variation linked to soil N, and yield selection implicate the same N-metabolism functions in maize. Although most candidates were analysis-specific, each analysis implicated a different enzyme in the aspartate-to-homoserine pathway. Candidates in each population also pointed to the Asp--Glu junction and to amino acid transport and protein turnover in ways consistent with each population's phenotype. These candidates require functional validation.

\subsection{Loci shared across amino acid families suggest protein turnover and ER--Golgi processing}

Few GWAS loci were shared across many amino acid phenotypes. The most interpretable case is the chromosome 4 locus at 174.57 Mb, associated with eight phenotypes (including Ala, Val, Lys, Phe, and Thr) from four biosynthetic families, and adjacent to \textit{Zm00001eb190800}, which encodes a peptidase A1 (Figure~\ref{fig:amino_locus_manhattan}). Peptidase A1 enzymes are pepsin-like aspartic proteases, named for the two aspartate residues in their active site rather than for any link to aspartate metabolism. Some aspartic proteases contribute to bulk protein turnover. In senescing tobacco leaves, the chloroplast aspartic protease CND41 degrades Rubisco, and plants with less CND41 retain more Rubisco under N limitation \citep{Kato2004CND41}. Because Rubisco breakdown is a major source of the amino acids released during leaf senescence \citep{Feller2008Rubiscolytics}, a protease of this kind could change the supply of amino acids to the grain, although A1 proteases also act in development and stress responses \citep{Soares2019AtypicalAP}. Still, a single locus affecting amino acids from four biosynthetic families is more easily explained by a change in protein degradation than by a change in one biosynthetic enzyme, which makes \textit{Zm00001eb190800} a plausible candidate for functional testing.

The chromosome 9 locus lies near two genes of the endomembrane system. A $\sim$13 kb region associated with seven phenotypes, including total free amino acids, spans \textit{Zm00001eb394360} and \textit{Zm00001eb394380}. Membrane proteins, including amino acid transporters, are made in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and pass through the Golgi apparatus on their way to the plasma membrane or vacuole \citep{Brandizzi2018ERGolgi}. \textit{Zm00001eb394360} encodes ER membrane protein complex subunit 4, part of a complex that inserts membrane proteins into the ER \citep{Guna2018EMC}. \textit{Zm00001eb394380} encodes rhomboid-like protein 19, a member of the rhomboid family of intramembrane proteases, and is annotated for transport from the Golgi back to the ER, the return route that recycles proteins between these compartments. The functions of most plant rhomboid-like proteins remain unknown \citep{Lavell2021RBL10}, and this annotation is predicted rather than tested. Both genes are predicted to act in the ER--Golgi system that processes many membrane proteins, including transporters involved in amino acid and N movement \citep{Brandizzi2018ERGolgi}, so any effect on the free amino acid pool would most likely be indirect. Because the associations do not clearly separate these genes from nearby genes, both loci should be treated as positional candidates, and kernel expression and mutant analyses are the most direct tests.

\subsection{The aspartate-to-homoserine pathway links soil-N adaptation, recurrent selection, and grain amino acid variation}

Asn was the most abundant free amino acid in the Goodman--Buckler panel, and because of its high N-to-carbon ratio, is a major N transport and storage form in plants \citep{Lea2007Asparagine}. It is synthesized from Asp by asparagine synthetase using the amide N of Gln, and catabolism returns carbon to Asp or oxaloacetate \citep{Lea2007Asparagine}. Asp is thus both the precursor of Asn and the entry point to the Asp-family pathway that produces Lys, Thr, Met, and Ile \citep{Han2021Aspartate, Wang2018AspSeeds, Viola2001AspartatePathway}. Pathway flux is regulated mainly by feedback inhibition of aspartate kinase by Lys and Thr, and of dihydrodipicolinate synthase by Lys \citep{Azevedo2006AspPathway, Jander2009AspArabidopsis}. The L-homoserine biosynthesis was implicated by all four analyses. It had a candidate gene in different branch points, had the lowest permutation-based empirical $P$ of the 468 CornCyc pathways, and was the only pathway that remained significant after correction (Figure~\ref{fig:pathway_recurrence}).

Across analyses, implicated genes spanned the Asp to homoserine pathway. Jarvis regions included aspartate kinase (\textit{Zm00001eb094670}), the first committed step \citep{Viola2001AspartatePathway}, and \textit{ask2} expression is linked to Met accumulation in developing kernels \citep{Duo2026Ask2}. The only soil N SNP significant in all three GWAS models, which also passed both tests for confounding with geography and climate, was near another aspartate kinase (\textit{Zm00001eb220040}). Indian Chief regions included aspartate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase (\textit{Zm00001eb214360}), and in the amino acid GWAS, homoserine dehydrogenase (\textit{Zm00001eb405410}), the first committed step to Thr and Met, was associated with Asn as a fraction of the Asp-family amino acids. Further downstream, Indian Chief regions included threonine synthase (\textit{Zm00001eb294790}) and methionine synthase (\textit{Zm00001eb052750}), the latter also being near a soil N SNP (S1\_265752163). In our RNA-seq of a maize hybrid sampled from flowering to after the black layer, three of the four L-homoserine pathway candidates, \textit{Zm00001eb094670}, \textit{Zm00001eb214360}, and \textit{Zm00001eb405410}, were expressed most in the kernel and cob and least in the leaf, and were expressed in the kernel throughout grain filling (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate_expression}, Supplemental Figure~S15, Supplemental Table~S23). Threonine synthase and the aspartate aminotransferase from Jarvis (\textit{Zm00001eb152450}) followed the same pattern.  The soil N aspartate kinase (\textit{Zm00001eb220040}) was not detected in this hybrid, whereas the other aspartate kinase (\textit{Zm00001eb094670}) was detected. Thus, \textit{Zm00001eb220040} may be expressed only in some genetic backgrounds, such as the landraces carrying the soil N allele, or in other tissues, stages, or N conditions.

Together, three populations with different germplasm and study designs point to the same pathway, with each analysis implicating a different enzyme, and L-homoserine biosynthesis reoccurred across analyses more often than expected by chance (Supplemental Figure S14). Most of these enzymes are also expressed in the developing kernel during grain filling, which is consistent with a role in grain amino acid composition, although expression alone does not demonstrate that their variation affects it. Drift could still contribute to the $F_{ST}$ candidate regions (Supplemental Figure~S13), and the soil-N alleles, although they came from many independent collection sites (Supplemental Figure~S8), may partly reflect shared ancestry. These genes are therefore strong candidates for direct tests. Because Asp is the precursor of Asn and exchanges amino groups with Glu, variation at the entry to the Asp-family pathway could shift how Asp-derived N is allocated among Asn, Asp-family amino acids, and the Glu/Gln pool. Links from Asp to Glu/Gln and the $\omega$-amidase pathway are discussed next.

\subsection{Candidate genes at the aspartate--glutamate junction link amino acid N with C--N recycling}

Glutamate exchanges amino groups with the TCA-cycle intermediate 2-oxoglutarate, which links N assimilation to carbon metabolism. Ammonium is assimilated mainly through the GS/GOGAT cycle. GS adds ammonium to Glu to form Gln, and GOGAT transfers the amide group of Gln to 2-oxoglutarate to form two Glu \citep{Hodges2002TwoOxoglutarate,MasclauxDaubresse2010NUE}. GDH interconverts Glu and 2-oxoglutarate, and in plants it acts mainly to deaminate Glu, which returns carbon to the TCA cycle when carbon is limited \citep{Miyashita2008GDH}. The concentration of 2-oxoglutarate serves as an indicator of carbon and N status \citep{Huergo2015TwoOG}. Together, Asp, Asn, Glu, and Gln make up most of the free amino acid pool and are major N transport forms in plants \citep{McAllister2016AAT}. Because no Glu/Gln pathway recurred across analyses, we discuss these junction candidates individually.

Similar to the aspartate homoserine pathway, each analysis implicated different genes at this junction. On the Asp side, Jarvis regions contained the aspartate aminotransferase \textit{Zm00001eb152450} (GOT1), which interconverts Asp and 2-oxoglutarate with oxaloacetate and Glu \citep{Han2021Aspartate, Zhou2009AspAT}. This gene was recently identified as \textit{THP3}, whose teosinte alleles improve N assimilation and seed protein in maize \citep{Huang2026THP3}. In contrast, overexpressing a cytosolic aspartate aminotransferase from alfalfa in \textit{Brassica napus} did not increase N use efficiency \citep{McAllister2016AAT}, suggesting that the effects of this enzyme depend on the isoform and the tissue in which it is expressed. The soil-N GWAS identified an $\omega$-amidase (\textit{Zm00001eb005710}). In Arabidopsis, ω-amidase hydrolyzes α-ketosuccinamate, the product of Asn transamination, to oxaloacetate and ammonia. It is also active on $\alpha$-ketoglutaramate, the product of Gln transamination, which yields 2-oxoglutarate \citep{Zhang2014OmegaAmidase}.

On the Glu side, the clearest case is Indian Chief (Figure~\ref{fig:ic_j_faa}). Most of the Cycle 14 increase in total free amino acids was Pro, so the lower Asp and Glu fractions likely reflect Pro accumulation. Indian Chief regions included two glutamate dehydrogenases (\textit{Zm00001eb060520}, \textit{Zm00001eb162300}), a glutamine synthetase (\textit{Zm00001eb162900}), and a class I glutamine amidotransferase (\textit{Zm00001eb217220}) that was also a candidate for free Pro in the amino acid GWAS. GDH mutants show altered amino acid profiles under carbon limitation \citep{Miyashita2008GDH, Dellero2020AminoNUE}, and cereal GS supplies Gln to developing grain \citep{Wei2021WheatGS, Fujita2022RiceGS13}. Because Pro is synthesized from Glu \citep{Alvarez2022ProHub}, increased flux from Glu to Pro, rather than increased primary N assimilation, is a plausible explanation, although enzyme activity needs to be measured. Glutamine amidotransferases transfer the amide N of Gln to many different acceptors, so \textit{Zm00001eb217220} cannot be assigned to the Glu/Gln cycle without functional evidence. However, rice \textit{FLOURY ENDOSPERM19}, which also encodes a class I glutamine amidotransferase, affects grain filling and carbon and N metabolism \citep{Lou2021FLO19}. Because most grain N is protein-bound, these shifts changed the soluble pool without affecting total grain amino acid N. Jarvis showed no corrected change, consistent with \citet{Moll1994}, in which its grain N gain came mainly from higher grain weight and post-anthesis N uptake. In the amino acid GWAS, GS/GOGAT genes (\textit{Zm00001eb329710}, \textit{Zm00001eb360480}, \textit{Zm00001eb059870}) were associated with ratio traits, including Asn/Glu. Thus, all three populations implicated genes at the junction where Asp and Glu exchange N with TCA-cycle carbon. These genes differ among analyses and lack significant pathway-level support, but finding the same junction in three independent designs makes it a priority for testing how maize balances amino acid N against carbon supply.

\subsection{Amino acid transport and protein turnover candidates}

Each analysis also contained candidates for amino acid transport or protein turnover, and these differed among populations in ways that mostly matched their phenotypes. They are individual candidates rather than recurring pathways, and because $F_{ST}$ windows in small populations can reflect drift \citep{Gerke2015DriftRRS}, we considered Indian Chief and Jarvis candidates only when they matched the amino acid phenotypes.

In Indian Chief, where Cycle 14 grain had more free amino acids but unchanged protein-bound amino acids, candidate regions included protein-turnover genes. \textit{Zm00001eb352300} is homologous to Arabidopsis \textit{ATG18a}, which is required for autophagosome formation during nutrient stress and senescence \citep{Xiong2005ATG18a}, and \textit{SAG39} (\textit{Zm00001eb214780}) is homologous to the senescence protease \textit{SAG12}, which localizes to senescence-associated vacuoles and supports N allocation to seeds \citep{Otegui2005SAV, James2018SAG12}. During senescence, autophagy and vacuolar proteases degrade leaf proteins, especially Rubisco, into amino acids exported via the phloem to developing seeds \citep{Feller2008Rubiscolytics, Have2017NRemobilization}. Additionally, maize autophagy mutants remobilize less N to grain \citep{Li2015MaizeAutophagy}. Higher protease or autophagy activity could increase amino acid supply and, if the supply exceeded storage-protein synthesis, enlarge the free amino acid pool without changing protein-bound amino acids, but these activities were not measured.

Jarvis regions instead contained genes for amino acid import and N signaling. An LHT-like gene (\textit{Zm00001eb132750}) is related to Arabidopsis LHT1, which imports amino acids into root and leaf cells \citep{Fan2023LHT}. In our  RNA-seq experiment, it was expressed at low levels, mainly in kernel and cob (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate_expression}B). Additionally, a ProT-like gene (\textit{Zm00001eb159580}), related to transporters of Pro, glycine betaine, and $\gamma$-aminobutyric acid (GABA) \citep{Lehmann2011ProT}, was expressed mainly in the root and stem (Supplemental Table S23). A tandem pair of CC-type glutaredoxin genes (\textit{Zm00001eb131830}, \textit{Zm00001eb131840}) lay in two of the most differentiated windows (ranked 2nd and 7th out of 2,151). Both were strongly downregulated under low nitrate in public maize leaf RNA-seq data (Supplemental Table S22) \citep{MaizExpressN, Mu2017LN, Guo2022LN, Ma2021LN, TorresRodriguez2021LN, AlonsoNieves2022LN, Wang2025NRT25}), and in Arabidopsis, CC-type glutaredoxins act in N signaling, including the mobile peptides CEPD1 and CEPD2 that increase nitrate uptake under N demand \citep{Patterson2016GRX, Ohkubo2017CEPD}. The glutaredoxins and the LHT-like gene also lie in the chromosome 3 Asp and Pro QTL interval of the B64 $\times$ CML14 population (Supplemental Figure~S5). Because Jarvis increased grain N mainly through post-anthesis N uptake \citep{Moll1994}, import and N-signaling genes fit its phenotype. Jarvis regions also contained NPL4-like protein 1 (\textit{Zm00001eb146560}), whose Arabidopsis homolog works with CDC48 to target ubiquitinated proteins, including the Rubisco large subunit, for degradation \citep{Li2022CDC48}, although this does not match the Jarvis phenotype.

In the amino acid GWAS, transporter candidates mapped to specific free amino acids. Three UmamiT exporters were associated with Gln (\textit{Zm00001eb379220}), Asp (\textit{Zm00001eb207630}), and Lys (\textit{Zm00001eb352400}). Seed-coat UmamiTs export maternal amino acids to the embryo \citep{Muller2015UmamiT}, so variation in export could change individual free amino acids without changing synthesis, although none of the three was detected in the kernel at any sampled stage of our RNA-seq analysis (Supplemental Table S23). The clearest kernel candidate was \textit{NPF4.6} (\textit{Zm00001eb277160}), associated with Lys as a fraction of the total free pool. Its family transports nitrate, peptides, amino acids, and hormones \citep{Leran2014NPF}, and it was expressed mainly in the kernel and cob during grain filling (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate_expression}B, Supplemental Figure S15). The Gln/Glu ratio mapped to the permease \textit{aaap40} (\textit{Zm00001eb261470}) near \textit{LHT1} and \textit{LHT2}, a cationic amino acid transporter (\textit{Zm00001eb050630}) was associated with branched-chain amino acids, and two vacuolar transporters, an \textit{AVT3A}-like gene (\textit{Zm00001eb429100}, Ile) and \textit{AVT6C} (\textit{Zm00001eb368680}, Asn), point to vacuolar storage \citep{Fujiki2017AtAVT3}. The \textit{AVT3A}-like gene was expressed in all tissues (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate_expression}B). Thus, transport and turnover candidates differed among populations, with protein turnover in Indian Chief, amino acid import and N signaling in Jarvis, and amino acid export, vacuolar storage, and a kernel-expressed NPF transporter in the amino acid GWAS, suggesting that each population varies at a different step of N flow to the grain.

\section{Conclusion}

Across three maize populations, candidate genes for grain amino acid variation fell into three groups. The first is the aspartate-to-homoserine pathway, the only pathway significant after correction, in which each analysis implicated a different step, aspartate kinase in Jarvis and the landrace soil-N GWAS, aspartate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase in Indian Chief, and homoserine dehydrogenase in the Goodman--Buckler panel. These steps may control how Asp-derived N is divided between Asn and the Asp-family amino acids. The second is the junction where Asp and Glu exchange N with TCA-cycle carbon, and the third is amino acid transport and protein turnover, with different genes in each population.

Each population captured different aspects of N in maize. Grain amino acid composition in the Goodman--Buckler panel, soil N at landrace collection sites, and N uptake and allocation in Indian Chief and Jarvis, which diverged under yield selection \citep{Moll1994}. Although none measured N flux directly, all implicated N-metabolism genes. Three of the four Asp-pathway genes were most highly expressed in the developing kernel and cob during grain filling. While enzyme functions are known, it remains untested whether the detected variants alter activity and thus grain amino acid composition. Pathway recurrence remained significant after correction only with stricter candidate sets, drift may explain some cycle differentiation, and we did not measure N uptake or remobilization. These candidates could be tested by combining Asp-pathway and amino acid transporter alleles in a common background and assaying kernel expression under varying N with $^{15}$N labeling to separate uptake from remobilization.


\section{Materials and methods}
Full details of all analyses are given in the Supplemental Methods.

\subsection{Plant material}

\noindent\textbf{Goodman--Buckler panel.} The Goodman--Buckler panel \citep{FlintGarcia2005Goodman} was grown and sampled as described by \citet{Shrestha2022}, and FAAs were measured in 280 of the 282 lines.

\noindent\textbf{B64 $\times$ CML14 population.} The B64 $\times$ CML14 mapping population was developed by crossing B64 and CML14, the Goodman--Buckler lines with the lowest and highest grain free Asn, respectively, and its F$_{2:3}$ families were grown and sampled as described in \citet{Fitzsimmons2026KernelKitchen}.

\noindent\textbf{Landraces.} We used 3,107 georeferenced maize landrace accessions from the CIMMYT International Germplasm Bank \citep{RomeroNavarro2017Landraces}, collected in 28 countries and territories across Mesoamerica (2,296), South America (667), and the Caribbean (144), for which soil N values were available at the collection sites.

\noindent\textbf{Indian Chief and Jarvis.} We used Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 of two open-pollinated populations, Indian Chief and Jarvis Golden Prolific, from a program of reciprocal recurrent selection for grain yield \citep{MollStuber1971,Moll1994}. For sequencing and amino acid profiling, Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 plants of both populations were grown in 2023 and 2025 at the Central Crops Research Station (Clayton, NC) in a randomized complete block design.

\subsection{Phenotyping}

\noindent\textbf{Amino acids.} PBAAs were quantified after acid hydrolysis by LC-MS/MS \citep{Yobi2018PBAA}. Hydrolysis converts Asn and Gln to Asp and Glu (reported as Asx and Glx) and destroys Trp and Cys, resulting in 15 PBAAs in the Goodman--Buckler panel and 16 in Indian Chief and Jarvis, where Gly was also quantified. All 20 FAAs were quantified by UPLC-MS/MS \citep{Gu2007LCMS}. Amino acid-derived N weighted each amino acid by its number of N atoms, with Asx and Glx assigned 1.5 N. Free and protein-bound pools were compared in the 279 Goodman--Buckler lines with both measurements \citep{Shrestha2022} by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, combining free Asp with Asn and free Glu with Gln to match Asx and Glx.

\noindent\textbf{Indian Chief and Jarvis comparisons.} Technical replicates were averaged within each biological sample (Indian Chief $n = 5$ and 8, Jarvis $n = 3$ and 8, for Cycle~0 and Cycle~14). Cycles were compared within each population by two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment within each family of tests (Supplemental Tables~S14--S17).

\noindent\textbf{Environmental variables.} Soil total N at 0--5 and 5--15~cm and soil pH and CEC at 0--5~cm were taken from SoilGrids 2.0 at 250-m resolution \citep{Poggio2021SoilGrids}, aridity from \citet{Zomer2022Aridity}, and latitude, longitude, and elevation from \citet{RomeroNavarro2017Landraces}, and each was assigned to the collection coordinates of each accession. Soil N at 0--5~cm was the primary GWAS phenotype, and 5--15~cm was used as a check.

\subsection{Genotyping}
We used public genotypes for the Goodman--Buckler panel (4,177,796 HapMap 3.2.1 SNPs \citealp{Bukowski2018HapMap3}, lifted from AGPv4 to B73 RefGen\_v5) and the landraces (495,755 genotyping-by-sequencing SNPs on RefGen\_v5 \citealp{RomeroNavarro2017Landraces}), and B64 $\times$ CML14 F$_2$ plants were genotyped as described in \citet{Fitzsimmons2026KernelKitchen}. For Indian Chief and Jarvis, 96 individuals per population--cycle group were skim sequenced (2~$\times$~150~bp reads, NovaSeq~6000), reads were aligned to RefGen\_v5 with BWA-MEM, and variants were called with GATK. After five low-quality samples were removed, 379 individuals remained, with a mean depth of 0.73$\times$ per individual.

\subsection{Heritability}
Broad-sense heritability ($H^2$) on a line-mean basis was estimated on the log scale with a linear mixed model including line, year, line $\times$ year, and block within year, and genomic heritability ($h^2$) using a VanRaden genomic relationship matrix for the 272 genotyped lines, along with a permuted-phenotype check. Consistency across years was measured by the Spearman correlation between 2017 and 2018 line means for the 251 lines grown in both years. For soil N, genomic heritability was estimated for the 3,107 landraces before and after removing 10 and 50 genotype principal components from the phenotype.

\subsection{Genome-wide association studies}

\noindent\textbf{Amino acid GWAS.} GWAS of the 78 amino acid phenotypes (Supplemental Table~S2) used phenotype BLUPs and the HapMap 3.2.1 SNPs in the HAPPI pipeline with the GAPIT MLM, MLMM, BLINK, and FarmCPU models \citep{Slaten2020HAPPI}. Significance was set at a 5\% Benjamini--Hochberg FDR for each phenotype and model, and MLMM gave no significant SNPs. Candidate genes were RefGen\_v5 genes within 25~kb of a significant SNP.
% TODO (ask Sarah): state the principal components and kinship used as covariates in the amino acid GWAS

\noindent\textbf{Soil N GWAS.} GWAS of soil N used MLM, BLINK, and FarmCPU with three genotype principal components as covariates and a 5\% Benjamini--Hochberg FDR for each model. Candidate genes were defined as for the amino acid GWAS.

\noindent\textbf{Tests for geographic confounding.} We ran the same GWAS on latitude, longitude, elevation, soil pH, CEC, and aridity and compared the overlap of significant SNPs with chance by hypergeometric tests. In a second, model-free test, every SNP was ranked genome-wide by the correlation between its allele dosage and each site variable. A soil N SNP was called linked to geography or climate if it ranked in the top 1\% for any non-N variable, not linked if it did not and ranked in the top 5\% for soil N, and inconclusive otherwise.

\subsection{QTL mapping}
BLUPs of free Asp and free Pro for the 213 F$_{2:3}$ families were mapped on the 610-marker genetic map of \citet{Fitzsimmons2026KernelKitchen} by Haley--Knott regression in R/qtl \citep{Broman2003}, with $\alpha = 0.05$ thresholds from 1,000 permutations and 1.5-LOD support intervals. Genes in the shared interval were taken from the RefGen\_v5 annotation (Supplemental Table~S8).

\subsection{Population-genomic analyses}
$F_{ST}$ between Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 was estimated in each population from genotype likelihoods with ANGSD \citep{Korneliussen2014ANGSD}, using a joint site-frequency spectrum estimated with \texttt{winsfs} \citep{Rasmussen2022winsfs} as the prior, and summarized in windows with GenWin \citep{Beissinger2015GenWin}. Windows in the top 5\% of each population were retained as candidate regions. These are empirical outliers, not significance tests. Candidate genes were annotated with PANTHER \citep{Thomas2022PANTHER} and Gene Ontology terms \citep{GO2026}. Population structure was inferred with NGSadmix \citep{Skotte2013NGSadmix} and PCAngsd \citep{Meisner2018PCAngsd}, and differentiation between Indian Chief and Jarvis was measured with Hudson's $F_{ST}$ \citep{Bhatia2013Fst}. $N_e$ was estimated by matching the observed genome-wide $F_{ST}$ between cycles to neutral Wright--Fisher simulations started from the Cycle~0 allele frequencies.

\subsection{Pathway recurrence}
Pathway membership was taken from CornCyc (Plant Metabolic Network release 2023, \citealp{Hawkins2021PMN}). The main test used Bonferroni-significant GWAS SNPs $\pm$25~kb and the top 3\% of windows from each $F_{ST}$ scan. For each of the 468 pathways, we counted how many of the four candidate sets contained a pathway gene and compared this count with 200,000 permutations that circularly shifted each set's intervals along each chromosome, preserving their number, size, and clustering. $P$ values were corrected across pathways with a minimum-$P$ procedure, and the test was repeated for 12 combinations of GWAS and $F_{ST}$ thresholds.

\subsection{Expression under contrasting N supply}
Expression responses of candidate genes to N supply were taken from MaizExpressN \citep{MaizExpressN}, a reanalysis of published maize RNA-seq studies (Supplemental Table~S22), including six leaf N-deficiency studies \citep{Mu2017LN, Guo2022LN, Ma2021LN, TorresRodriguez2021LN, AlonsoNieves2022LN, Wang2025NRT25}.
