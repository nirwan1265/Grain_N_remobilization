\begin{abstract}
Grain nitrogen accumulation in maize reflects coordinated uptake, assimilation, transport, storage, and remobilization of nitrogen into free and protein-bound amino acid pools. Yet the genetic links between environmental nitrogen adaptation and grain amino acid composition remain unclear. We combined amino acid profiling, genome-wide association studies (GWAS), environmental GWAS, biparental QTL mapping, and recurrent-selection population genomics to dissect grain nitrogen metabolism across complementary genetic contexts. In the Goodman–Buckler Association Panel, mature grain free amino acid (FAA) composition was dominated by asparagine and proline. GWAS of amino acid traits identified loci tied to amino acid transport, Glu/Gln metabolism, Asp-family metabolism, redox regulation, and protein turnover. In a georeferenced maize landrace panel, soil-N GWAS highlighted loci including an omega-amidase/NIT2-like CN hydrolase and an aspartate/glutamate/uridylate kinase-domain gene, linking historical soil nitrogen to Asn/Gln and Asp-family metabolism. 
To connect these signals with selection-driven nitrogen partitioning, we profiled Indian Chief and Jarvis populations after 14 cycles of yield selection that produced contrasting grain and stover N dynamics. Indian Chief Cycle 14 showed a marked expansion of the FAA pool, increased FAA-derived nitrogen, and strong proline enrichment with reduced Asp and Glu fractions, whereas Jarvis showed weaker changes, including a nominal increase in Asp fraction. Low-pass sequencing and population-genomic scans revealed population-specific differentiation at loci annotated for amino acid transport, autophagy, ubiquitin–proteasome activity, proteolysis, Glu/Gln cycling, and Asp-family metabolism. B64 × CML14 biparental mapping identified a shared chromosome 3 QTL for Pro and Asp, implicating endosperm protein trafficking via a SAR1A candidate near the QTL peak. Collectively, these results support a model in which maize grain nitrogen variation arises not from a single conserved locus but from distributed variation affecting interconnected pathways related to Asp-derived nitrogen flux, Glu/Gln-mediated C–N recycling, protein turnover, and intracellular transport during kernel development
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\lettrine[lines=2]{\color{color2}N}{itrogen} (N) is essential for maize growth, specifically kernel development, but its use in agriculture presents a major sustainability challenge. Modern crop production relies heavily on N fertilizer, yet much of the applied N is not recovered by crops and is lost via leaching, runoff, or gaseous emissions (\cite{OjedaRivera2025NitrogenEfficientMaize}). Improving nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) therefore requires understanding how plants acquire inorganic N from soil, assimilate it into organic compounds, transport it among tissues, and remobilize it into developing grain \citep{MasclauxDaubresse2010NUE,Have2017NRemobilization}. In maize kernels, N is stored mainly in amino acid pools, as either free amino acids (FAAs) or protein-bound amino acids (PBAAs), making grain amino acid composition a direct biochemical readout of grain N allocation.

Plant N metabolism incorporates N uptake, assimilation, transport, and recycling. Nitrate and ammonium are assimilated primarily via the GS/GOGAT cycle, which incorporates ammonium into glutamine and regenerates glutamate using 2-oxoglutarate as a carbon skeleton \citep{MasclauxDaubresse2010NUE,Hodges2002TwoOxoglutarate}. Thus, Glu/Gln metabolism lies at the interface of N assimilation and central carbon metabolism. Asparagine and glutamine are major N-rich transport and storage compounds, while glutamate and aspartate act as central amino-group donors and C–N metabolic hubs \citep{Bernard2009CytosolicGS,MasclauxDaubresse2010NUE}. Aspartate is especially important because it feeds the aspartate-family pathway, which produces methionine, threonine, lysine, and isoleucine, and also connects to asparagine, NAD, and nucleotide metabolism \citep{Viola2001AspartatePathway,Wang2018AspSeeds,Han2021Aspartate}.

Grain N accumulation also depends on N remobilization from vegetative tissues during reproduction. Leaf senescence is a regulated process in which chloroplast proteins, nucleic acids, and other N-rich macromolecules are degraded and exported to developing sinks \citep{Have2017NRemobilization,Sakuraba2022NStarvationSenescence}. Protein breakdown proceeds via chloroplast degradation, vacuolar and autophagic pathways, and the ubiquitin--26S proteasome system \citep{Liu2008ProteinDegradationNRemobilization,Have2017NRemobilization}. The resulting amino acids and ammonium are converted into mobile N forms, transported through source--sink pathways, and stored in grains. Cytosolic glutamine synthetase is central to this recycling because it reassimilates ammonium into glutamine, a major mobile organic N form \citep{Bernard2009CytosolicGS}. Proline adds a stress-linked amino acid axis. In maize, water stress can modify proline accumulation or export from senescing leaves in a genotype-specific way, while glutamine and asparagine remain the main N compounds in leaf exudates \citep{Carceller1999ProlineRemobilization}. Therefore, changes in grain FAA composition should be interpreted as remodeling across multiple N-related amino acid pools, including Asn/Gln transport forms, Asp/Glu C--N metabolic hubs, and Pro linked metabolism, rather than as direct evidence of increased total N remobilization alone.


Although N uptake, amino acid metabolism, and senescence-associated remobilization are well studied, they are usually examined separately. It remains unclear whether the same N-metabolism functions recur across long-term environmental adaptation, natural variation in grain amino acid composition, and short-term selection for contrasting grain–stover N partitioning. Maize is well suited for such integrative analysis. The Goodman–Buckler Association Panel captures broad allelic diversity for mapping complex traits \citep{FlintGarcia2005Goodman}, georeferenced landrace panels enable tests of genetic associations with environmental gradients such as soil N \citep{RomeroNavarro2017Landraces}, and the Indian Chief and Jarvis populations add a selection layer, as 14 cycles of reciprocal recurrent selection for grain yield produced contrasting N-remobilization patterns in grains and stover \citep{Moll1994}.

We integrate grain amino acid profiling, grain amino acid GWAS, soil-N environmental GWAS, population-genomic scans of Indian Chief and Jarvis, and an exploratory B64 × CML14 biparental mapping population developed for grain asparagine variation. We analyze free and protein-bound amino acid pools, focusing on asparagine, aspartate, glutamate, glutamine, and proline because of their roles in N transport, C–N recycling, amino acid storage, and stress-linked N metabolism. Across these layers, we test whether genetic, and selection signals converge on shared processes underlying grain N remodeling. Our results are consistent with a model in which maize grain N variation involves related processes including Asp-family metabolism, Glu/Gln-mediated C–N recycling, amino acid transport, protein turnover, and intracellular trafficking during kernel development.






\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Kernel free amino acid composition in the Goodman--Buckler panel is dominated by asparagine and proline}

Free amino acid (FAA) composition was evaluated across 280 of the 282 lines in the Goodman--Buckler panel grown in four replicates (two years $\times$ two blocks) at the Genetics Farm near Columbia, Missouri (Supplementary Tables S1--S2). To assess relative abundance, amino acids were ranked within each genotype based on average values across replicates. Asparagine and proline consistently showed the highest relative abundance across the panel (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_faa_asn_pro_rank}A--B). Asparagine ranked as the most abundant amino acid in 146 of 280 genotypes (52\%), while proline ranked first in 118 genotypes (42\%). Notably, asparagine and proline comprised the top two amino acids in 179 of 280 genotypes (64\%), regardless of order (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_faa_asn_pro_rank}B). This rank dominance reflected large differences in abundance: median genotype-mean concentrations varied 144-fold across the 20 FAAs, from asparagine and proline (highest) to cysteine and methionine (lowest) (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_faa_asn_pro_rank}C). Abundance and variability were nevertheless decoupled (Supplementary Table S3). Methionine showed the greatest genotypic variability (CV 180\%) despite low abundance, followed by glutamine (CV 130\%), whereas glutamate, tryptophan, and glycine varied the least (CV 44--54\%). Asparagine and proline were moderately variable (CV 70\% and 63\%; median 72\% across the 20 FAAs) yet still spanned 116-fold and 34-fold across the 280 genotype means, indicating substantial genotypic variation in both.

Amino acid phenotypes were repeatable. Median broad-sense heritability ($H^2$) was 0.71 across 40 FAA traits (range 0.14--0.88) and 0.53 across 76 protein-bound amino acid (PBAA) traits (range 0.09--0.83), so most of the variation among line means reflects genotype rather than plot-to-plot noise. The most heritable individual FAAs were lysine (0.88), asparagine (0.83), aspartate (0.81), and proline (0.80). Therefore, the free amino acids that are most abundant and most variable across the panel are also the most reliably measured. Genomic heritability estimated from the marker-based relationship matrix gave medians of 0.47 for FAA and 0.50 for PBAA traits, confirming that a substantial share of this variation is additive and therefore accessible to association mapping (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_heritability}; Supplementary Table S4).

To quantify the free pool's contribution to grain amino acid nitrogen, we compared genotype-level FAA and PBAA across matched taxa ($n = 279$; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_gb_faa_pbaa_metrics}, Supplementary Table S5) \citep{Shrestha2022}. The protein-bound pool dominated, exceeding the free pool 47-fold for total amino acids and 42-fold for amino acid-derived N (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, all $P < 10^{-40}$). FAAs, therefore, contributed only 2.4\% of estimated grain amino acid-derived N. Pool composition, nevertheless, differed. Because acid hydrolysis converts asparagine to aspartate and glutamine to glutamate, protein-bound measurements resolve only Asx and Glx. Tryptophan and cysteine are destroyed, and glycine was not reported. Thus, the protein-bound pool includes 15 amino acids compared to 20 in the free pool. To compare the two pools on the same basis we used matched denominators throughout: free Asp+Asn and Glu+Gln were combined, and glycine, tryptophan, and cysteine --- 8.4\% of the free pool --- were excluded from the free-pool total. On this basis proline was 23.1\% of FAAs compared with 10.6\% of PBAAs, and (Asx + Glx + Arg + Lys + His) was 56.0\% of the free pool against 26.5\% of the protein-bound pool. Because the matched fraction necessarily bundles aspartate with asparagine and glutamate with glutamine, it includes residues carrying a single nitrogen. Restricted to unambiguously N-rich residues, which only the free pool resolves, asparagine, glutamine, arginine, lysine, and histidine together accounted for 35.7\% of FAAs. Composition variability across genotypes also differed. The proline fraction varied by 44\% (FAA) against 8\% (PBAA), and the combined amide and basic fraction by 20\% against 6\%. Thus, the free pool is a small but compositionally labile and highly repeatable fraction of grain amino acid nitrogen, dominated by asparagine and proline and enriched for nitrogen-rich residues, whereas protein-bound composition varies far less across the panel.

\subsection{Association mapping links abundant, heritable free amino acids to amino acid metabolism and transport genes}

To identify loci affecting grain amino acid composition, we scanned 78 amino acid–derived phenotypes (Supplementary Tables S1--S2), including absolute abundance of 20 individual FAAs; six biosynthetic-family composites (IMNTDK, EHPRQ, SGC, WFY, LAV, IVL); 20 ratios to the total free pool; 25 ratios to the family composite; four ratios between amino acids (N.D, N.E, N.Q, Q.E); and three whole-pool measures (total FAA, total PBAA \citep{Shrestha2022}, and estimated nitrogen).

At a Benjamini--Hochberg false discovery rate of 5\%, applied per phenotype and model, we found 2,461 significant SNPs across 71 phenotypes. After lifting SNP positions from AGPv4 to B73 RefGen\_v5, 1,811 were within 25 kb of a gene, yielding 3,693 candidate genes (Supplementary Table S6); merging significant SNPs within 25 kb of each other gave 2,152 loci (Supplementary Table S7). Most candidate genes were specific to one phenotype (3,002 of 3,693), and per phenotype their number was similar across biosynthetic families (71, 69 and 67 for aspartate, glutamate and serine phenotypes), so family-level gene counts mainly reflect how many phenotypes each family contributes. Full results (Manhattan plots, QQ plots, annotations) are at \url{https://j83gk2-nirwan-tandukar.shinyapps.io/amino_gwas_app/}.

Few loci were shared across phenotypes. Three loci were significant for nine phenotypes: chromosome 4 at 212.55 Mb ($P = 1.5 \times 10^{-18}$; no gene within 25 kb), chromosome 7 at 40.80 Mb (\textit{Zm00001eb306240}, a K homology domain protein) and chromosome 9 at 133.93 Mb (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_amino_chr9_locus}). Each spans multiple biosynthetic families and the free pool, and none is near a gene annotated for amino acid metabolism or nitrogen transport. Because composites and ratios share amino acids, phenotype counts overstate independent signals; at chromosome 9, the nine phenotypes resolve into three SNP groups not in linkage disequilibrium. The next locus, chromosome 4 at 174.57 Mb, was significant for eight phenotypes, including five amino acids from four families (alanine, valine, lysine, phenylalanine and threonine), and lies next to \textit{Zm00001eb190800}, encoding peptidase A1 (aspartic protease) (Fig.~\ref{fig:amino_locus_manhattan}). Three linked SNPs ($r^2 = 0.92$--$0.94$) 2.6--6.1 kb upstream were significant for six phenotypes, and the strongest association ($P = 6.0 \times 10^{-15}$, \textit{A}) lies 5.9 kb downstream.

We examined individual amino acids, focusing on abundant, reliably measured FAAs (Table~\ref{tab:amino_gwas_focus}). Lysine, asparagine, aspartate, and proline were most heritable (line-mean $H^2 = 0.80$--$0.88$), with genomic heritability ranging from 0.40 (proline) to 0.84 (aspartate) (Supplementary Table S3). Asparagine, the most abundant FAA ($H^2 = 0.83$, $h^2_g = 0.68$), had the most loci (356 across abundance and five ratios) and the most trait-matched candidates (Methods), including two asparagine synthetase domain genes (\textit{Zm00001eb036060}, \textit{Zm00001eb036070}; \textit{N.E}), a glutamine amidotransferase type 2 gene (\textit{Zm00001eb329710}; \textit{N.E}, $P = 6.1 \times 10^{-10}$), homoserine dehydrogenase (\textit{Zm00001eb405410}; \textit{N.IMNTDK}, SNP within the gene), and lysine-branch enzymes dihydrodipicolinate synthase and reductase (\textit{Zm00001eb389290}, \textit{Zm00001eb176170}). An AVT6C amino acid transporter (\textit{Zm00001eb368680}) was significant for asparagine ($P = 1.3 \times 10^{-8}$) and the aspartate-family composite ($P = 2.5 \times 10^{-11}$) in both BLINK and FarmCPU. Lysine ($H^2 = 0.88$, $h^2_g = 0.73$) mapped within \textit{Zm00001eb277160} (NRT1/PTR family 4.6; \textit{K.Total}, $P = 2.5 \times 10^{-11}$, both models) and 15 kb from NADH-dependent glutamate synthase (\textit{Zm00001eb360480}; \textit{K.IMNTDK}, $P = 2.8 \times 10^{-11}$). Aspartate ($H^2 = 0.81$, $h^2_g = 0.84$) had 91 loci (20 for abundance), with one trait-matched gene: a WAT1-related (UmamiT) gene 0.4 kb from a \textit{D.IMNTDK} SNP (\textit{Zm00001eb207630}; $P = 2.7 \times 10^{-10}$).

Proline, the second most abundant FAA, was nearly as repeatable as asparagine ($H^2 = 0.80$) but had lower genomic heritability ($h^2_g = 0.40$), indicating that markers capture less among-line variation, and had fewer loci (59; all for the absolute trait). Its only trait-matched gene, a class I glutamine amidotransferase (\textit{Zm00001eb217220}), contains the significant SNP ($P = 2.2 \times 10^{-9}$) and falls in a top-5\% $F_{ST}$ window in Indian Chief (Supplementary Table S19). Glutamine, the second most variable FAA (CV 130\%) with high genomic heritability (0.85), showed the strongest amino-acid association in Table~\ref{tab:amino_gwas_focus} ($P = 4.9 \times 10^{-22}$, \textit{Q.E}); trait-matched genes included a WAT1-related (UmamiT) gene containing the significant SNP (\textit{Zm00001eb379220}; \textit{Q}, $P = 1.2 \times 10^{-11}$) and three amino acid transporter genes on chromosome 6 (\textit{Zm00001eb261470}--\textit{Zm00001eb261490}; \textit{Q.E}). Methionine, the most variable FAA (CV 180\%), had 104 loci but no trait-matched candidate gene; the high CV reflects a strongly skewed across-line distribution (198-fold range; Supplementary Table S3).

\subsection{Soil nitrogen environment GWAS of maize landraces points to candidate genes in aspartate-family and amide nitrogen metabolism}

The Goodman--Buckler analysis identified 3,693 candidate genes for grain amino acid composition, most of them specific to a single phenotype. Because these results reflect standing variation in a temperate inbred panel under managed fertility, we asked whether maize landraces sampled across a soil nitrogen gradient implicate the same genomic regions, which would suggest shared loci responding to field nitrogen availability.

Because a soil-N GWAS reflects historical environments rather than grain phenotypes, we interpreted it cautiously. Our goal was not to equate soil-N adaptation with grain amino acid composition, but to ask whether independent ecological and grain-biochemical signals implicate similar N-metabolism functions. We associated the Romero Navarro maize landrace panel \citep{RomeroNavarro2017Landraces} with total soil N at 0--5~cm and 5--15~cm at each collection site \citep{Batjes2006ISRICWISE5ArcMin}. Depths were strongly correlated across 3,107 accessions ($r=0.84$; Fig.~\ref{fig:soilN_gwas}A), so we used 0--5~cm as the main phenotype and 5--15~cm as a check. Landraces span Mexico through South America and a wide soil-N range (Fig.~\ref{fig:soilN_gwas}B). A major limitation is that soil N is site-level: 3,107 accessions correspond to only 467 distinct soil-N values. Consequently, soil-N genomic heritability was $\sim$1.00 (permuted $h^2\le 0.05$) and remained 1.00 after removing 10 genotype PCs and 0.98 after removing 50 (0.88 at 5--15~cm; Fig.~\ref{fig:soilN_gwas}C), indicating soil N is largely predicted by genome-wide ancestry. PC covariates cannot fully address this: the three PCs used explain only 2.01\% of genotypic variance due to diffuse structure in a geographically continuous panel (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_pca_scree}). We therefore treated confounding by relatedness and geography as the primary interpretive risk and tested it directly.

We ran a soil-N GWAS using MLM, BLINK, and FarmCPU, testing 495,755 markers with three genotype PCs and a 5\% Benjamini--Hochberg FDR threshold (Fig.~\ref{fig:soilN_gwas}D). We identified 234 significant SNPs (MLM 2, BLINK 218, FarmCPU 30); 15 appeared in at least two models and one in all three. Of the 234, 16 were within 25~kb of an FDR-significant SNP from the Goodman--Buckler amino acid GWAS, similar to the 11.9 expected given 5.1\% genome coverage (binomial $P = 0.14$), and all 16 came from a single model. Thus, the panels share no loci beyond chance, so we compare them below by gene function rather than genomic position.

To test whether these associations reflect geography or climate rather than soil N, we used two approaches. First, we ran GWAS for other site variables (latitude, longitude, elevation, soil pH, CEC, aridity, and soil N at 5--15~cm) and counted SNP overlap with soil N (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:soilN_specificity}; Supplementary Table~S9). Only 2 of 234 soil-N SNPs (0.9\%) were also significant for latitude, longitude, or elevation, similar to the 0.7 expected by chance ($P=0.14$), whereas geographic variables shared 18.2\% and 15.0\% of SNPs with each other (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:soilN_specificity}A). Soil N instead overlapped strongly with soil N at 5--15~cm (51 vs.\ 0.1 expected), aridity (38 vs.\ 0.2), soil pH (13 vs.\ 0.1), and CEC (3 vs.\ 0.2) (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:soilN_specificity}B). Because the two soil-N depths are highly correlated, this overlap indicates consistency rather than independent replication. Overall, 190/234 soil-N SNPs were not significant for any other variable (Supplementary Table~S10), including 14/15 detected by at least two models. Second, for every SNP we correlated genotype with each site variable across 3,107 accessions and ranked each SNP genome-wide (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:soilN_geno_env}; Supplementary Table~S11). Of the 234 soil-N SNPs, 31 (13.2\%) ranked in the top 1\% for at least one geographic variable, versus 1.8\% genome-wide ($P=1.1\times10^{-17}$). However, SNPs selected solely by genotype--soil N correlation showed much higher geographic signal (35.9\%), indicating that GWAS SNPs are substantially less geography-confounded than correlation alone would suggest ($P=5.9\times10^{-15}$; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:soilN_geno_env}B; Supplementary Table~S12). Of 15 multi-model SNPs, three were primarily environment-linked (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{suppfig:soilN_geno_env}A). The strongest, S9\_151742636 (5~kb from \textit{Zm00001eb399680}, TatC), was the top GWAS hit ($P = 2.6\times10^{-13}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:soilN_gwas}D) and ranked in the top 0.001\% for correlation with aridity and soil pH; because it was also significant in pH and aridity GWAS, we classified it as climate-linked rather than soil-N. S6\_161051050 (\textit{Zm00001eb289040}; aridity, top 0.95\%) and S5\_47128668 (no gene within 25~kb; CEC, top 0.2\%) were excluded similarly. Five SNPs (including both SNPs near \textit{Zm00001eb220040} and the $\omega$-amidase SNP) correlated strongly with soil N and with no other variable. The remaining seven were inconclusive: their soil-N correlations were weak (outside the top 5\%), and their GWAS signal appeared only in BLINK and FarmCPU; none was significant in other environmental GWAS using the same models. Overall, 12 SNPs detected by at least two models passed both tests (Table~\ref{tab:soilN_specific}).

Two SNPs map near nitrogen-metabolism genes. S5\_22338979 lies 4.4~kb upstream of \textit{Zm00001eb220040}, an amino acid kinase-domain gene annotated with aspartate kinase activity (GO:0004072), the first step of the aspartate-derived amino acid pathway. It is the only soil-N SNP significant in all three models, is also significant at 5--15~cm, and a nearby SNP 41~bp away (S5\_22338938) is significant in two models. Its genotype ranked in the top 1\% for soil N at both depths and outside the top 20\% for all other site variables. S1\_16128632 lies within \textit{Zm00001eb005710} ($\omega$-amidase/NIT2-like CN-hydrolase) and ranked in the top 0.2\% for soil N and outside the top 15\% for every other variable. Both SNPs are rare (minor allele frequency 0.030 and 0.016), so their associations rest on a small number of accessions. The strongest SNP, S8\_138640842 ($P = 3.5\times10^{-11}$), lies within \textit{Zm00001eb356170} (methionine S-methyltransferase); it was supported only by BLINK and FarmCPU and was inconclusive in the correlation test. At 5--15~cm, S8\_133179205 lies 0.1~kb from \textit{Zm00001eb354640} (S-adenosylmethionine synthase) and was not linked to other site variables (aridity strongest; top 8.7\%). These gene links are annotation-based hypotheses.

Two additional loci may affect regulation or trafficking. S3\_54622531, significant in two models at both depths, is 17.9~kb from \textit{Zm00001eb130210}, a RING/FYVE/PHD zinc-finger ubiquitin ligase; its rice homolog \textit{TTL1}/\textit{OsRFPV-7} influences thermotolerance and grain size \citep{Lin2023TTL1}. It passed both specificity tests. S9\_129109559 lies within \textit{Zm00001eb393130} (vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein 52A). Because Arabidopsis Vps52/POK localizes to the Golgi and mediates vesicle trafficking and polar growth \citep{Lobstein2004Vps52}, this locus could connect soil N to protein sorting and nutrient redistribution, but it is significant in only one model and is the weakest candidate.

\subsection{Free amino acid profiles of Indian Chief and Jarvis show population-specific remodeling of the soluble amino acid pool}

To connect the Goodman--Buckler amino acid GWAS and soil-N environmental GWAS with a selection-based population framework, we next examined the Indian Chief and Jarvis maize populations. These populations provide an independent system for testing whether short-term selection altered grain amino acid pools and N allocation because Indian Chief and Jarvis served as open-pollinated base populations in a reciprocal recurrent-selection experiment for grain yield over 14 cycles \citep{Moll1994}. The original study showed that reciprocal recurrent selection altered N allocation differently between the two populations. At nonlimiting N supply, Jarvis Cycle~14 accumulated greater grain N, driven primarily by greater post-anthesis N uptake from the soil, while apparent N remobilization from stover to grain remained largely unchanged relative to the starting Jarvis population. In contrast, Indian Chief Cycle~14 showed increased post-anthesis stover biomass, lower post-anthesis N loss from stover, and no significant increase in grain N relative to the original Indian Chief population \citep{Moll1994}. These contrasting outcomes are consistent with broader maize NUE studies showing that grain N gains can reflect both improved N recovery from the soil and altered internal N allocation during grain filling \citep{Mueller2019NUE}. Therefore, we interpret the Indian Chief and Jarvis amino acid profiles as evidence of grain amino acid pool remodeling and N allocation differences, rather than as direct evidence for increased senescence-driven N remobilization.

For FAA profiling, representative Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 individuals from Indian Chief and Jarvis were grown at the Central Crops Research Station in Clayton, North Carolina, under standard field management practices. Each biological sample was measured in three technical replicates, which were averaged before testing, giving five Cycle~0 and eight Cycle~14 samples for Indian Chief and three and eight for Jarvis. Within each population we compared the two cycles with two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (normal approximation with continuity correction) on eight pre-specified FAA metrics (total FAA per mg sample; the proline, asparagine, aspartate, glutamine and glutamate fractions of total FAA; the N-rich FAA pool, Asn + Gln + Arg + Lys + His, per mg; and FAA-derived N per mg), with Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) correction across the eight (Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_j_faa}; Supplementary Table~S13). Indian Chief showed significant remodeling of both total FAA abundance and FAA composition. Total FAA per mg increased from 1.40 in Cycle~0 to 2.46 in Cycle~14 (Wilcoxon rank-sum $P = 0.0043$; BH-adjusted $P = 0.017$; Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_j_faa}A), with no overlap between the two cycles. Within this expanded pool, proline became more dominant, increasing from 0.320 to 0.519 of total FAA ($P = 0.016$; BH-adjusted $P = 0.025$). In contrast, the acidic amino acids decreased as fractions of the total FAA pool: aspartate from 0.115 to 0.075 ($P = 0.016$; BH-adjusted $P = 0.025$) and glutamate from 0.140 to 0.062 ($P = 0.0068$; BH-adjusted $P = 0.018$), while the asparagine and glutamine fractions did not change (Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_j_faa}B). Thus, Indian Chief Cycle~14 showed both increased total FAA abundance and a compositional shift away from Asp/Glu fractions and toward proline enrichment. Across all 20 individual FAAs, the isoleucine and valine fractions also differed nominally, but no single amino acid remained significant after correction across the 20 (BH-adjusted $P = 0.063$; Supplementary Table~S14). The proline enrichment is notable because the one trait-matched candidate for proline in the Goodman--Buckler GWAS, \textit{Zm00001eb217220}, lies in a top-5\% $F_{ST}$ window in Indian Chief (Supplementary Table~S19).

Jarvis showed no statistically supported changes, but this comparison had little power: with three Cycle~0 samples, the smallest $P$ the rank-sum test can return (normal approximation) is 0.019. The aspartate fraction increased from 0.075 in Cycle~0 to 0.137 in Cycle~14 and reached that minimum ($P = 0.019$; the two cycles did not overlap), but it did not survive correction (BH-adjusted $P = 0.15$). Total FAA per mg, the proline and glutamate fractions, the N-rich FAA pool and FAA-derived N did not differ between cycles (Supplementary Table~S13). The lack of significant change in Jarvis therefore reflects limited sampling at least as much as stability of the FAA pool.

To test whether FAA compositional remodeling was accompanied by changes in amino acid-derived N pools, we converted FAA and PBAA measurements to N-equivalent values by weighting each amino acid by its N content (Asx and Glx, which are not resolved after acid hydrolysis, at 1.5 N) and normalizing by sample mass. These values represent amino acid-derived N pools and not total elemental grain N as measured in the original recurrent-selection study \citep{Moll1994}. Protein-bound amino acid composition did not change between cycles in either population: no PBAA metric, and no individual protein-bound amino acid, differed even nominally (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_pbaa_profiles}; Supplementary Tables~S14--S15). In Indian Chief, FAA-derived N per mg increased significantly from Cycle~0 to Cycle~14 (1.75 to 2.99; Wilcoxon rank-sum $P = 0.0043$; BH-adjusted $P = 0.013$), whereas PBAA-derived N and total amino acid-derived N per mg did not change ($P = 0.42$ for both; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_ic_j_aa_n_pools}; Supplementary Table~S16), suggesting that the Indian Chief response was concentrated in the soluble FAA pool rather than in the much larger protein-bound amino acid pool. This distinction is important because the FAA-derived N increase occurred alongside proline enrichment and reduced Asp/Glu fractions, indicating compositional remodeling of soluble amino acid N rather than a uniform increase across all grain amino acid pools. This interpretation is consistent with the Goodman--Buckler FAA--PBAA comparison, where the two pools differed strongly in scale and composition, with the free pool richer in proline and in amide and basic amino acids (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_gb_faa_pbaa_metrics}; Supplementary Table~S5). Jarvis showed no significant changes in FAA-derived, PBAA-derived, or total amino acid-derived N per mg.

\subsection{Skim sequencing reveals that Indian Chief and Jarvis diverged during reciprocal recurrent selection}

We performed skim sequencing on 379 individuals from the Indian Chief and Jarvis populations at Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 (Indian Chief 96 and 92; Jarvis 96 and 95). Mean sequencing depth was 0.73$\times$ (median 0.72$\times$, range 0.32--1.91$\times$), but differed between cycles: Cycle~14 samples were sequenced more deeply than Cycle~0 samples in both populations (Indian Chief 0.61$\times$ vs.\ 0.88$\times$; Jarvis 0.68$\times$ vs.\ 0.76$\times$; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_seq_sample}A). At this coverage, the number of genotypes called per sample depends directly on depth (median 240,979 of 512,667 SNP genotypes missing per sample; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_seq_sample}B), so analyses based on hard-called genotypes would confound sequencing depth with cycle. We therefore based all population-structure analyses on genotype likelihoods. After filtering, 512,667 SNPs were retained. Their allele-frequency spectrum was dominated by rare variants (median minor allele frequency 0.021), and the transition/transversion ratio was consistent across the ten chromosomes (2.88--3.08; genome-wide 2.95; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_seq_variant}).

Unsupervised ancestry estimation (NGSadmix, $K = 2$--5) on 82,061 sites thinned to one per 10~kb separated the four population--cycle groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_j_structure}A). The likelihood improved markedly up to $K = 4$, where independent runs converged and each group formed its own cluster; at $K = 5$, runs disagreed and the gain in likelihood was small. At $K = 2$ and 3, Indian Chief Cycle~0 individuals were modelled as a mixture of the Jarvis Cycle~0 and Indian Chief Cycle~14 clusters, placing the ancestral Indian Chief population between Jarvis and its own selected descendant. A genotype-likelihood PCA (PCAngsd) showed the same pattern (Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_j_structure}B): PC1 (14.3\% of variance) separated Indian Chief Cycle~14 from Jarvis Cycle~14, with both Cycle~0 groups near the centre, and PC2 (6.7\%) separated Cycle~14 from Cycle~0. Thus, the two populations, which were less differentiated at Cycle~0, moved apart in opposite directions over 14 cycles.

Genetic differentiation between the populations confirmed this divergence. $F_{ST}$ between Indian Chief and Jarvis, estimated from genotype likelihoods, nearly doubled from 0.111 at Cycle~0 to 0.211 at Cycle~14, and increased on all ten chromosomes (Cycle~0, 0.079--0.134; Cycle~14, 0.169--0.245; Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_j_structure}C). Because Cycle~14 was sequenced more deeply, which should give more accurate allele frequencies and, if anything, lower $F_{ST}$, this increase is unlikely to reflect sequencing depth. Divergence is the expected outcome of reciprocal recurrent selection, which selects each population for its performance in combination with the other and so favours complementary rather than shared alleles \citep{Comstock1949RRS}; genetic drift in two small, separately maintained populations pushes them apart as well. Over 14 cycles, Indian Chief and Jarvis therefore kept their separate identities while becoming more genetically distinct from each other and from their own ancestral generations.

\subsection{$F_{ST}$ scans identify population-specific candidate regions in Indian Chief and Jarvis}

Before scanning for selection, we asked how much of the allele-frequency change between cycles could be explained by genetic drift. In the reciprocal recurrent selection program, each cycle was formed by intermating the self-pollinated progenies of the 20 best plants per population \citep{MollStuber1971}, which implies an effective population size ($N_e$) of at most 20 per cycle. Genome-wide mean $F_{ST}$ between Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 was 0.095 in Indian Chief and 0.114 in Jarvis, values reproduced by neutral Wright--Fisher simulations at $N_e \approx 30$ and $N_e \approx 26$, respectively (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_ne_drift}). These estimates are of the same order as, though slightly above, the value implied by the breeding design, indicating that most of the genome-wide change between cycles is consistent with drift in small populations. At this $N_e$, drift moves long linked regions together, so individual outlier regions cannot be formally distinguished from drift. We therefore treat the scans below as exploratory.

We scanned each population for the regions with the largest allele-frequency change between Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 using $F_{ST}$ summarized in spline-defined windows (2,162 windows in Indian Chief and 2,151 in Jarvis; median length 500~kb). Windows in the top 5\% of each population's $F_{ST}$ distribution ($F_{ST} \geq 0.157$ in Indian Chief and $\geq 0.178$ in Jarvis; 109 and 108 windows) were retained as candidate regions and contained 2,001 and 1,179 genes, respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_j_fst}; Supplementary Tables~S17--S20). Candidate regions were largely population-specific. Window $F_{ST}$ was only weakly correlated between the two populations (Spearman $r = 0.19$), and only five regions (2.5~Mb containing 73 genes) were in the top 5\% in both. This overlap was no greater than expected by chance: circular shifts of the Jarvis scan along the genome, which preserve the size and clustering of candidate windows, gave a mean overlap of 4.4~Mb ($P = 0.80$; Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_j_fst}). Thus, the two populations show no evidence of shared targets of differentiation, as expected if the changes in each population reflect independent drift together with population-specific responses to selection. Of the 2,152 Goodman--Buckler amino acid GWAS loci, 192 (8.9\%) lie in a top-5\% window of either population, matching the 192.9 expected from circular shifts of the loci along the genome ($P = 0.52$; the windows cover 8.9\% of the genome), so the two analyses do not converge on the same regions beyond chance.

Each candidate set contains more than 1,000 genes, so genes from almost any metabolic pathway are expected to be present by chance. We therefore describe the following genes as annotations of interest rather than as evidence that a given pathway was selected. In Indian Chief, candidate regions included genes of Asp-family amino acid metabolism, \textit{Zm00001eb214360} (aspartate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase) and \textit{Zm00001eb294790} (threonine synthase), and genes of Glu/Gln interconversion, including a glutamate dehydrogenase (\textit{Zm00001eb060520}), an amino acid dehydrogenase (\textit{Zm00001eb162300}), a glutamine synthetase domain-containing protein (\textit{Zm00001eb162900}), and a glutamine amidotransferase domain-containing protein (\textit{Zm00001eb217220}), which also contains a significant SNP for proline in the Goodman--Buckler GWAS (Table~\ref{tab:amino_gwas_focus}). Candidate regions also contained genes involved in N transport and protein turnover: the senescence-specific cysteine protease \textit{SAG39} (\textit{Zm00001eb214780}) and an ammonium transporter (\textit{Zm00001eb214890}), which lie in the same window on chromosome~5; an NRT1/PTR-family transporter (\textit{Zm00001eb287950}) and the autophagy-related gene \textit{ATG18g} (\textit{Zm00001eb287690}), which share a window on chromosome~6; and two further autophagy-related genes (\textit{Zm00001eb352300} and \textit{Zm00001eb372060}). These annotations are compatible with the Indian Chief FAA phenotype, in which total FAA and the Pro fraction increased while the Asp and Glu fractions decreased from Cycle~0 to Cycle~14, but they do not establish that these genes underlie it.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec12}

The results support a model in which maize grain N variation arises through multiple genetic pathways that implicate related biological functions. Across soil-N environmental GWAS, grain amino acid GWAS, Indian Chief and Jarvis population-genomic scans, and exploratory B64 $\times$ CML14 biparental mapping, most candidate genes were specific to individual datasets, yet their annotations consistently involved connected N-remobilization and amino acid metabolism processes. We therefore interpret the study as consistent with shared functional themes rather than single-locus control. Candidate genes supporting this view are listed in Supplementary Table S21, and the conceptual model is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fig7_integrative_synthesis}. The following sections examine the main recurring functional modules and their implications for grain amino acid composition and N allocation.

\subsection{Multi-trait GWAS candidates suggest regulatory and trafficking components of grain amino acid variation}

Among the Goodman--Buckler amino acid GWAS candidates, \textit{Zm00001eb393450} had the highest occurrence across significant trait associations, appearing in eight amino acid or amino acid-derived traits: alanine (\textit{A}), glutamate-total (\textit{E.Total}), \textit{EHPRQ}, branched-chain amino acid traits (\textit{IVL} and \textit{LAV}), serine (\textit{S}), threonine (\textit{T}), and total amino acid abundance. It is annotated as a transcriptional regulatory protein AlgP-like and also falls within a lysine-rich arabinogalactan-protein family. Its repeated association across multiple amino acid traits makes it biologically compelling but must be viewed with caution. In cassava, an AlgP-annotated gene, \textit{Sc18g015190}, was identified by ionomic GWAS as a phosphorus-accumulation candidate, and promoter polymorphisms at this locus were associated with altered promoter activity, transcript abundance, and P accumulation \citep{Yin2022CassavaIonomics}. Functional silencing of \textit{Sc18g015190} further reduced chlorophyll and P content under low-P conditions, supporting a role for this AlgP-annotated gene in nutrient homeostasis \citep{Yin2022CassavaIonomics}.

A broader starvation-response context is supported by bacterial AlgP studies, where AlgP acts as a DNA-binding, histone H1-like protein linked to polyphosphate granule organization during nitrogen starvation \citep{Chawla2022AlgPPolyP}. Polyphosphate itself provides relevant nutrient-stress context as an ancient phosphate- and energy-storage polymer involved in stress responses and cellular regulation \citep{Achbergerova2011PolyP}. Although this bacterial function should not be directly transferred to maize, it conceptually supports the idea that AlgP-like or lysine-rich regulatory proteins may contribute to nutrient-linked cellular organization and stress responses. In the Goodman--Buckler panel, the association of \textit{Zm00001eb393450} with multiple amino acid traits suggests that this locus may influence broad variation in grain amino acid balance through regulatory or cellular-homeostasis mechanisms rather than a single biosynthetic reaction. Thus, \textit{Zm00001eb393450}, together with other recurrent trafficking-related GWAS candidates, provides a useful target set for future validation.

Beyond the AlgP-like gene, GWAS identified candidates annotated for Golgi or anterograde transport, Golgin-like function, and nucleotide-sugar transport, including UDP-galactose/glucose transporter-like genes. One example was \textit{Zm00001eb137680}, a Golgin candidate associated with five amino acid traits (\textit{IVL}, \textit{S}, \textit{V}, \textit{W}, and \textit{Y}). In Arabidopsis, \textit{MAG4}/\textit{Atp115}, a Golgi-localized tethering factor, is required for efficient ER-to-Golgi transport of seed storage-protein precursors, and \textit{mag4} mutants accumulate 12S globulin and 2S albumin precursors in dry seeds \citep{Takahashi2010MAG4}. Thus, although these maize GWAS candidates require validation, their recurrence across multiple amino acid traits supports grain amino acid variation reflecting not only biosynthetic-enzyme differences but also trafficking processes. Additional candidate-gene annotations, trait associations, and model-specific GWAS results are available through the Shiny application at \url{https://j83gk2-nirwan-tandukar.shinyapps.io/amino_gwas_app/}.


\subsection{Aspartate- and asparagine-pathway genes recur across soil-N adaptation, recurrent selection, and grain amino acid variation}

Aspartate is both a proteinogenic amino acid and an N-bearing metabolic hub. Its carbon skeleton supplies asparagine and the aspartate-family amino acids lysine, threonine, methionine, and isoleucine, and its amino group connects it to the glutamate pool through transamination \citep{Han2021Aspartate,Wang2018AspSeeds,Viola2001AspartatePathway}. Each of our analyses, which measured a different N-related phenotype in a different population, identified genes from this network. These genes were different in each analysis rather than a single shared locus, and we therefore describe them as candidates rather than as evidence that the pathway as a whole was selected.

The recurrent-selection populations provided genes at consecutive steps of the aspartate-family pathway. In Jarvis, the top-5\% $F_{ST}$ regions included \textit{Zm00001eb094670}, aspartate kinase, which catalyzes the first committed step of the pathway, converting aspartate to aspartyl phosphate \citep{Viola2001AspartatePathway,Wang2018AspSeeds}; expression of maize \textit{ask2} has been associated with methionine accumulation in developing kernels \citep{Duo2026Ask2}. In Indian Chief, the top-5\% regions included \textit{Zm00001eb214360}, aspartate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase, which acts immediately downstream of aspartate kinase; \textit{Zm00001eb294790}, threonine synthase, at the end of the threonine branch; and \textit{Zm00001eb052750}, cobalamin-independent methionine synthase, which also lay within 25~kb of a soil-N GWAS SNP (Supplementary Tables~S19--S20). Because an effective population size of about 25--30 allows drift alone to produce candidate regions of this size (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_ne_drift}), these genes are candidates for selection rather than demonstrated targets.

The free amino acid profiles of the two populations changed in different ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:ic_j_faa}; Supplementary Table~S13). In Indian Chief, total free amino acids and FAA-derived N per mg of grain increased from Cycle~0 to Cycle~14, the proline fraction increased, and the Asp and Glu fractions decreased, whereas protein-bound amino acids and total amino acid-derived N per mg did not change. In Jarvis, no FAA metric changed after multiple-testing correction; the Asp fraction increased from 0.075 to 0.137, but this was significant only before correction and rests on three Cycle~0 samples. These patterns are consistent with the original selection experiment, in which reciprocal recurrent selection increased grain N per plant in Jarvis, largely through greater grain weight, but did not significantly change grain N in Indian Chief \citep{Moll1994}. Total grain N is dominated by protein-bound amino acids, whereas free amino acids form a small soluble pool. The Indian Chief response therefore suggests a remodeling of soluble amino acid N, away from free Asp and Glu and toward proline, rather than greater N accumulation in the grain.

The Goodman--Buckler grain amino acid GWAS identified genes at other steps of the same network. A SNP within \textit{Zm00001eb405410}, homoserine dehydrogenase, which converts aspartate-semialdehyde to homoserine at the branch point toward threonine and methionine, was associated with the Asn fraction of the aspartate-family pool ($P = 3.0 \times 10^{-9}$). On the lysine branch, \textit{Zm00001eb389290}, 4-hydroxy-tetrahydrodipicolinate synthase, lay within the LD interval of a SNP associated with the Asn/Gln ratio, and \textit{Zm00001eb176170}, 4-hydroxy-tetrahydrodipicolinate reductase, lay within 25~kb of a SNP associated with the Asn/Glu ratio. On the asparagine side, two asparagine synthetase domain-containing genes (\textit{Zm00001eb036060} and \textit{Zm00001eb036070}) lay within 25~kb of a SNP associated with the Asn/Glu ratio. Several of these associations involve asparagine ratios, consistent with asparagine being a major transport and storage form of N that draws on the aspartate pool.

The soil-N GWAS provided an ecological rather than a grain-level link. The most robust soil-N association, the only SNP significant in all three GWAS models, which was also significant for soil N at 5--15~cm and was not more strongly correlated with any other environmental variable tested (Supplementary Tables~S10--S11), lies 4.4~kb upstream of the transcription start of \textit{Zm00001eb220040}, a gene with an aspartate/glutamate/uridylate kinase domain annotated with aspartate kinase activity, making it a candidate through possible cis-regulation. A second soil-N candidate, the omega-amidase \textit{Zm00001eb005710}, links asparagine catabolism to the glutamate network and is discussed below. Because soil N covaries with other features of landrace environments, and its connection to grain amino acid composition is indirect, these associations point to aspartate metabolism in adaptation to soil N without bearing directly on grain composition. Together, the aspartate- and asparagine-pathway candidates from the four analyses are hypotheses that can be tested directly, for example by comparing aspartate-family amino acids in lines carrying alternative alleles at \textit{Zm00001eb405410}, \textit{Zm00001eb094670}, and \textit{Zm00001eb214360}. Aspartate is tied to the Glu/Gln pool through aminotransferase and amidotransferase reactions, which we consider next.


\subsection{Glu/Gln metabolism links aspartate-derived N with C--N recycling}

Two candidates sit at the junction between the aspartate and glutamate pools. In Jarvis, the top-5\% $F_{ST}$ regions included \textit{Zm00001eb152450}, aspartate aminotransferase (glutamate--oxaloacetate transaminase, GOT1), which reversibly converts aspartate and 2-oxoglutarate into oxaloacetate and glutamate and can shift the relative sizes of the Asp and Glu pools \citep{Han2021Aspartate,Zhou2009AspAT}. GOT1 was recently identified as \textit{THP3}, a gene whose teosinte alleles enhance N assimilation and seed protein accumulation in maize \citep{Huang2026THP3}. In the soil-N GWAS, a SNP within \textit{Zm00001eb005710}, an omega-amidase (NIT2), was significant in two models. Omega-amidase completes the asparagine and glutamine transaminase pathways, hydrolyzing 2-oxosuccinamate to oxaloacetate and 2-oxoglutaramate to 2-oxoglutarate with the release of ammonia \citep{Zhang2014OmegaAmidase}, and thereby returns amide N from asparagine and glutamine to the aspartate and 2-oxoglutarate pools.

In Indian Chief, the top-5\% $F_{ST}$ regions contained several glutamate- and glutamine-metabolism genes: \textit{Zm00001eb060520}, glutamate dehydrogenase; two NADP-specific glutamate dehydrogenase family genes (\textit{Zm00001eb162300} and \textit{Zm00001eb214770}); and \textit{Zm00001eb162900}, a glutamine synthetase catalytic domain-containing protein. Plant glutamate dehydrogenase connects glutamate with 2-oxoglutarate and ammonium; it is not the main route for primary ammonium assimilation, but GDH-deficient mutants show reduced glutamate catabolism and altered amino acid profiles under carbon limitation \citep{Miyashita2008GDH,Dellero2020AminoNUE}. Glutamine synthetase incorporates ammonium into glutamine, and cereal GS isoforms contribute to N remobilization and to the supply of glutamine to developing grain \citep{Wei2021WheatGS,Fujita2022RiceGS13}. Together with the reduced Glu fraction and increased proline fraction in Indian Chief Cycle~14 grain, these candidates are consistent with altered Glu/Gln cycling and glutamate catabolism rather than increased primary N assimilation.

The grain amino acid GWAS identified genes of the GS/GOGAT cycle. Two glutamate synthase (NADH-GOGAT) family genes were associated with aspartate-family traits: a SNP within the LD interval of \textit{Zm00001eb329710} was associated with the Asn/Glu ratio ($P = 6.1 \times 10^{-10}$), and \textit{Zm00001eb360480} lay within 25~kb of a SNP associated with the lysine fraction of the aspartate-family pool ($P = 2.8 \times 10^{-11}$). A glutamine synthetase domain-containing gene, \textit{Zm00001eb059870}, lay within 25~kb of a SNP associated with the isoleucine fraction of the branched-chain pool. GS and GOGAT together assimilate and reassimilate ammonium into glutamate, the amino-group donor for aspartate and asparagine synthesis.

One gene was identified by both the selection scan and the GWAS. \textit{Zm00001eb217220}, a class-I glutamine amidotransferase domain-containing protein, lay in an Indian Chief top-5\% $F_{ST}$ region, and a SNP within the gene was associated with free proline in the Goodman--Buckler panel ($P = 2.2 \times 10^{-9}$), the amino acid whose fraction increased most in Indian Chief after selection. Glutamine amidotransferases transfer the amide N of glutamine in many biosynthetic pathways, so this gene should not be interpreted as a Glu/Gln-cycle enzyme without functional evidence; however, rice \textit{FLOURY ENDOSPERM19}, a class-I glutamine amidotransferase, affects grain filling and C--N metabolism \citep{Lou2021FLO19}. Overall, the recurrent-selection scans, the soil-N GWAS, and the grain amino acid GWAS each implicate components of the network that links aspartate, asparagine, glutamate, and glutamine, but through different genes, consistent with distributed variation in grain N metabolism rather than a single shared locus.

\subsection{N transport and protein-turnover candidates recur across selection scans and grain amino acid GWAS}

Beyond Asp-family metabolism, the candidate sets also highlight N transport, amino acid transport, and protein turnover as recurring themes. These signals do not imply that the same loci were recovered across all datasets. Instead, they suggest that distinct candidates in different populations and mapping panels may affect related processes involved in N movement, amino acid redistribution, proteostasis, and source--sink allocation.

In Indian Chief, \textit{Zm00001eb214890}, homologous to the Arabidopsis ammonium transporter \textit{AtAMT2}, may influence ammonium movement or redistribution \citep{Neuhaeuser2009AtAMT2}. Indian Chief also carried \textit{Zm00001eb287950}, an \textit{NRT1/PTR}-family candidate. Related transport signals were also detected in the Goodman--Buckler amino acid GWAS, including \textit{Zm00001eb287980}, associated specifically with glutamine (\textit{Q}), and \textit{Zm00001eb277160}, associated with several asparagine- and aspartate-related ratio traits. The \textit{AVT3A}-like candidate \textit{Zm00001eb429100} was associated with glutamate (\textit{E}), alanine (\textit{A}), glycine (\textit{G}), and the LAV family composite. Because Arabidopsis AtAVT3 proteins mediate vacuolar amino acid export, this candidate extends the transport signal to intracellular amino acid redistribution \citep{Fujiki2017AtAVT3}.

%QTL stuff in next paragraph
Jarvis contributed a complementary set of amino acid transport candidates, including \textit{Zm00001eb132750}, an LHT-like transporter related to a rice amino acid transport family \citep{Fan2023LHT}; \textit{Zm00001eb159580}, a PROT-like transporter related to Arabidopsis proline/compatible-solute transporters \citep{Lehmann2011ProT}; and \textit{Zm00001eb389560}, a \textit{GLUTAMINE DUMPER}-family candidate linked to amino acid export in Arabidopsis \citep{Pratelli2012GDU}. Amino acid transporters \textit{AAP2} (Zm00001eb138820) and \textit{AAP6} (Zm00001eb138780) were also identified in the shared Asp and Pro QTL. Notably, \textit{AAP6} has been shown to regulate grain protein content\citep{Wang2022}, supporting a role for amino acid transport in determining nitrogen allocation to developing kernels. These candidates suggest that both recurrent selection and natural grain amino acid variation may involve changes in the movement of inorganic N and organic N among tissues, cells, and intracellular storage pools.

A second recurring theme was protein turnover and proteostasis. This connection was strongest in Indian Chief, where \textit{Zm00001eb352300}, homologous to Arabidopsis \textit{ATG18a}, points to autophagosome formation under nutrient stress and senescence \citep{Xiong2005ATG18a}. Indian Chief also contained \textit{Zm00001eb214780}, a senescence-specific cysteine protease related to \textit{SAG12}, which has been associated with N allocation during senescence and seed production \citep{James2018SAG12}. The localization of SAG12 to senescence-associated vacuoles with strong proteolytic activity further supports a role for senescence-related proteolysis in nutrient recycling \citep{Otegui2005SAV}. Consistent with this theme, the Goodman--Buckler GWAS identified candidates related to macroautophagy, ER-associated degradation, RING-type E3 ubiquitination, autophagosome assembly, and senescence regulation. Jarvis also contained \textit{Zm00001eb210720}, a 26S proteasome regulatory-particle candidate homologous to rice \textit{OsRpn5}, which is best interpreted as a proteostasis candidate rather than direct evidence for N remobilization \citep{Shibahara2002Proteasome}.

Together, the transport and protein-turnover candidates suggest mechanisms for N release, movement, and intracellular redistribution. However, these processes alone do not explain how released N is metabolically reassimilated. The Glu/Gln-related candidates provide this second layer, linking amino acid turnover to C--N recycling and ammonium reassimilation.

\subsection{Proline remodeling and a shared Pro/Asp QTL implicate amino acid pool partitioning}

Proline showed one of the clearest amino acid phenotypes in the recurrent-selection populations. In Indian Chief, Cycle 14 had an expanded total FAA pool and a strongly enriched proline fraction, while Asp and Glu fractions declined. This pattern indicates that selection shifted the balance of soluble amino acid pools rather than uniformly increasing all FAA components. Because proline can act as a stress-linked amino acid and a flexible N-containing metabolite, its enrichment may reflect changes in amino acid storage, turnover-derived FAA accumulation, or source--sink N partitioning. However, we interpret this pattern cautiously, as the Indian Chief result alone does not reveal the biochemical pathway underlying proline enrichment or demonstrate increased total grain N remobilization.

The B64 $\times$ CML14 mapping result provides an independent genetic link to this proline-centered pattern. Although B64 and CML14 were selected for contrasting grain asparagine rather than proline, the biparental scan identified a shared chromosome~3 QTL for free proline and aspartate, with both traits peaking at the same marker. This overlap suggests that Pro and Asp variation can share genetic control in at least one segregating background, even though they represent different biochemical axes. Together with the Indian Chief FAA shift, this supports the idea that proline remodeling is part of the broader grain N-remodeling network rather than an isolated phenotype.

A nearby candidate gene at the shared Pro/Asp QTL peak, \textit{Zm00001eb127670}, encodes a GTP-binding protein SAR1A, a small GTPase that initiates COPII vesicle formation and mediates ER-to-Golgi protein export. In rice endosperm, simultaneous knockdown of \textit{OsSar1a/b/c} disrupts storage-protein trafficking, causing proglutelin accumulation in the ER, reduced mature glutelin, altered $\alpha$-globulin deposition, and floury/shrunken seeds \citep{Tian2013Sar1}. Thus, although \textit{SAR1A} is not a canonical proline or aspartate biosynthetic enzyme, its location at the shared Pro/Asp QTL peak suggests a plausible connection between endomembrane trafficking, storage-protein deposition, and free amino acid balance in maize kernels.

This candidate was not in the top 5\% $F_{ST}$ outlier set in either recurrent-selection population. In Indian Chief, the window containing \textit{Zm00001eb127670} had an $F_{ST}$ of 0.07329, below the 5\% cutoff of 0.15684. In Jarvis, the same gene was close to but still below the empirical cutoff, with an $F_{ST}$ of 0.17827 compared with a 5\% cutoff of 0.17831. We therefore do not treat the selection scans as independent support for \textit{SAR1A}. Instead, \textit{SAR1A} is nominated based on its position within the shared Pro/Asp QTL interval and its plausible role in ER-to-Golgi trafficking, and it remains to be tested by fine-mapping and developing-kernel expression analysis.

Overall, the shared Pro/Asp QTL supports the central conclusion that grain amino acid variation may involve not only biosynthesis but also protein trafficking, turnover, and nitrogen redistribution during kernel development. The \textit{SAR1A} candidate could affect free Pro and Asp indirectly by altering how amino acids are partitioned between soluble and storage-protein-bound pools in the endosperm. Fine-mapping and developing-kernel expression analysis will be needed to test whether variation at \textit{Zm00001eb127670} underlies the shared Pro/Asp QTL.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec13}
%Sarah is still working on this 

By integrating soil-N environmental GWAS, grain amino acid GWAS, recurrent-selection population genomics, and biparental QTL mapping, we show that maize grain nitrogen composition is associated with variation across multiple, partially overlapping biological pathways rather than control by a single conserved locus or a small number of major-effect loci.

\quad Across independent datasets, we identify candidate genes falling into four broad functional categories: (1) aspartate-family metabolism, which governs allocation of nitrogen into essential amino acid biosynthetic pathways; (2) Glu/Gln-centered C--N cycling, linking nitrogen assimilation and reassimilation with central carbon metabolism; (3) amino acid transport and intracellular partitioning; and (4) protein turnover and recycling through autophagy- and ubiquitin-mediated pathways. Support for the relevance of these processes comes from GWAS signals for amino acid transporters, dehydrogenases, and Asp-family enzymes; soil-N associations implicating asparagine, glutamine, and aspartate-related metabolism in environmental adaptation; selection-driven FAA remodeling and population-specific differentiation in Indian Chief and Jarvis; and a shared Pro/Asp QTL implicating ER-to-Golgi trafficking.

\quad Importantly, the recurrent-selection populations demonstrate that substantial shifts in grain amino acid composition can arise through distributed allele-frequency changes across these pathways. Indian Chief exhibited expansion of the FAA pool, increased FAA-derived N, and strong proline enrichment with reduced Asp and Glu fractions, while PBAA-derived and total amino acid-derived N pools did not change significantly. This indicates that the strongest Indian Chief response was concentrated in the soluble amino acid pool rather than reflecting a uniform increase across all grain amino acid pools. In contrast, Jarvis showed weaker phenotypic shifts but contained candidate loci directly linked to aspartate-family biosynthesis. These contrasting outcomes suggest that similar selection pressures may reshape grain nitrogen allocation through distinct genetic routes within a partly shared metabolic network.

Since the Indian Chief and Jarvis populations were advanced via reciprocal recurrent selection based on hybrid performance rather than fully isolated selection, they should not be viewed as completely independent. Although they did not directly mix, selection decisions relied on their cross performance, creating a shared breeding context. Moreover, with 14 selection cycles, some allele-frequency changes and outliers may reflect drift or other stochastic effects rather than selection alone. We therefore treat the Indian Chief–Jarvis contrasts as complementary evidence for selection-associated remodeling of grain nitrogen traits, while acknowledging that drift and the reciprocal selection design may also have contributed to the observed genomic differentiation.

\quad Together, these results support a model in which maize grain nitrogen variation is governed by distributed modulation of N flux, protein turnover, amino acid transport, and intracellular trafficking during kernel development. In this framework, FAA and PBAA pools are not passive storage forms but dynamic products of coordinated metabolic and cellular processes. Perturbation of aspartate-family enzymes should shift the balance of nitrogen among downstream amino acid pools; manipulation of glutamate dehydrogenase, glutamine synthetase, or aminotransferases should alter C--N recycling and FAA accumulation; and disruption of protein trafficking or autophagy pathways should affect the partitioning between soluble and storage-protein-bound amino acids. Future functional studies targeting these processes will be critical to resolving causal mechanisms.

\quad From an applied perspective, our findings suggest that optimizing maize grain N allocation while maintaining agronomic performance will likely require coordinated modification of multiple pathways rather than targeting a single locus. Candidate targets include regulators of Asp-family flux, Glu/Gln cycling enzymes, amino acid transporters, and components of protein turnover and trafficking systems. By identifying recurrent functional themes across candidate sets, this study provides a framework for breeding and engineering strategies aimed at improving nitrogen use efficiency and reducing fertilizer-associated nitrogen losses in maize production systems \citep{OjedaRivera2025NitrogenEfficientMaize}.

\subsection{Phenotypic Evaluation}
\subsubsection{Grain/Stover Nitrogen}

Grain and stover N concentrations for all Indian Chief (IC) and Jarvis (J) lines were previously determined for the base generation (G$_0$) and the 14th cycle of selection (G$_{14}$) by Moll \textit{et al.}~\citep{Moll1994}. At a high fertilizer rate of $224~\mathrm{kg~N~ha^{-1}}$, J$_{14}$ produced heavier kernels and had higher grain N concentration than the original J$_0$ line. Post-anthesis N uptake from the soil supplied nearly all of this additional grain N, whereas the amount of N remobilized from stover to grain remained largely unchanged.

Indian Chief showed a contrasting response. In IC$_{14}$, vegetative biomass increased, but grain weight and grain N concentration remained similar to IC$_0$. The loss of N from stover after flowering was lower than in the base generation, indicating that more N was retained in stems and leaves and less was transferred to the grain. Thus, 14 cycles of selection increased grain N in Jarvis primarily through greater post-anthesis N uptake, whereas Indian Chief retained more N in vegetative tissue and showed little change in grain N concentration~\citep{Moll1994}.

Because soil N is an environmental estimate rather than a plant measurement, GWAS results were interpreted as historical associations with soil N.

\subsection{Population-genomic analyses}

\subsubsection{Genotype-likelihood framework}

Population-genomic statistics were estimated using ANGSD v0.941 \citep{Korneliussen2014ANGSD}, which is appropriate for low-coverage sequencing because it uses genotype likelihoods rather than hard genotype calls. Analyses were performed separately for Indian Chief Cycle~0 (IC\_C0), Indian Chief Cycle~14 (IC\_C14), Jarvis Cycle~0 (J\_C0), and Jarvis Cycle~14 (J\_C14). The same read- and base-level filtering parameters were applied across all population--cycle groups to ensure comparable estimates among contrasts.

For each population--cycle group, site allele-frequency likelihoods were estimated with ANGSD and stored as SAF files. For pairwise analyses, joint two-dimensional site-frequency spectra were estimated from the corresponding SAF files using \texttt{winsfs}, a window-EM SFS estimator, to improve computational efficiency. These joint spectra were then used as priors for downstream $F_{ST}$ estimation.

\subsubsection{Differentiation between cycles}

Allele-frequency change between Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 was measured separately in Indian Chief (IC\_C0 versus IC\_C14) and Jarvis (J\_C0 versus J\_C14) with $F_{ST}$. For each contrast, a joint two-dimensional site-frequency spectrum was estimated from the SAF files with \texttt{winsfs} \citep{Rasmussen2022winsfs} and used as the prior for \texttt{realSFS fst index}. Per-site $F_{ST}$, the ratio of the per-site numerator and denominator reported by \texttt{realSFS fst print}, was summarized along each chromosome with the GenWin smoothing-spline method (\texttt{splineAnalyze}; smoothness 500~kb, method~4) \citep{Beissinger2015GenWin}, which places window boundaries at inflection points of a spline fitted to $F_{ST}$ rather than at fixed intervals. This produced 2,162 windows in Indian Chief and 2,151 in Jarvis (median length 500~kb), each summarized by the mean per-site $F_{ST}$. Windows at or above the 95th percentile of the window $F_{ST}$ distribution of each population were retained as candidate regions. These are empirical outliers rather than significance thresholds: at the effective population size of these populations, drift moves linked regions together, and no neutral distribution was derived for individual windows.

To compare candidate regions between populations, whose window boundaries differ, the genome was divided at every window boundary of either population, and each resulting segment was assigned the $F_{ST}$ of the Indian Chief window and of the Jarvis window that contained it. Overlap was measured as the number of base pairs (in 10-kb bins) in the top 5\% in both populations and compared with 10,000 circular shifts of the Jarvis candidate track along the concatenated chromosomes, which preserve the size and clustering of candidate windows. Concordance of window $F_{ST}$ between populations was summarized with Spearman's correlation across segments.

\subsubsection{Effective population size}

In the reciprocal recurrent selection program, each cycle was formed by intermating, within each population, the self-pollinated progenies of the 20 plants whose half-sib testcross progenies with the other population had the highest grain yield \citep{MollStuber1971}. Testcross progenies were used only for evaluation, so no alleles were exchanged between populations, and each cycle involved a single sampling of parents from the previous cycle. Neutral allele-frequency change under this scheme is therefore expected to follow a Wright--Fisher process with one generation per cycle and an effective population size ($N_e$) of at most 20, the value reached when all selected parents contribute equally.

To compare the observed differentiation with this expectation, we estimated $N_e$ for each population from the allele-frequency change between Cycle~0 and Cycle~14. Per-site allele frequencies for each population--cycle group were taken from ANGSD \citep{Korneliussen2014ANGSD}, retaining sites with data for at least 30 individuals in both cycles and with the same major and minor alleles in both cycles. Sites were grouped into non-overlapping 250-kb windows (at most 400 randomly chosen sites per window; 8,524 windows in Indian Chief and 8,527 in Jarvis), Hudson's $F_{ST}$ \citep{Bhatia2013Fst} between Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 was computed per window as a ratio of sums, and the genome-wide mean was taken across windows. We then simulated neutral Wright--Fisher drift for 14 generations, starting each site at its observed Cycle~0 frequency, for $N_e$ = 20, 30, 50, 75, 100, 150, 250, and 500. At both time points, simulated frequencies were resampled using the number of individuals with data at each site, and $F_{ST}$ was computed with the same estimator as for the observed data. $N_e$ was taken as the value at which the simulated genome-wide mean $F_{ST}$ matched the observed value, interpolating linearly between grid points. Because the simulation does not model linkage, it was used only to estimate genome-wide $N_e$ and not to test individual windows.

\subsubsection{Population structure, principal component analysis, and between-population differentiation}

Because sequencing depth differed between cycles, population structure was inferred from genotype likelihoods rather than hard-called genotypes. Phred-scaled genotype likelihoods (GATK \texttt{PL}) for all 379 individuals were taken from the filtered VCF and converted to Beagle genotype-likelihood format; individuals without reads at a site were given uninformative likelihoods. Sites were retained if at least 50\% of individuals had reads and the likelihood-weighted minor allele frequency was at least 0.05, and were thinned to one site per 10~kb to reduce linkage, leaving 82,061 sites. Admixture proportions were estimated with NGSadmix \citep{Skotte2013NGSadmix} for $K = 2$--5 with three independent seeds per $K$; $K$ was chosen as the largest value at which runs converged to the same likelihood and solution. Principal component analysis was performed on the genotype-likelihood covariance matrix estimated with PCAngsd \citep{Meisner2018PCAngsd} from the same sites. Differentiation between Indian Chief and Jarvis was estimated separately at Cycle~0 and Cycle~14 from joint two-dimensional site-frequency spectra estimated with \texttt{winsfs} \citep{Rasmussen2022winsfs} from the per-group ANGSD SAF files. Hudson's $F_{ST}$ \citep{Bhatia2013Fst} was computed from each spectrum as a ratio of averages over all sites, per chromosome and genome-wide.

\subsubsection{Genome-wide association Studies}

\paragraph{Environment GWAS}

% \rra{this section requires much mor detail an information. See comments in the results about the nitrogen phenotype used. "Approximately 495,755 SNPs for maize landraces" this sentence is missing a verb. Genome-wide association analysis for soil N was performed in the maize landrace panel \citep{RomeroNavarro2017Landraces} using the 3,107 accessions with both genotype and soil N data and 495,755 SNPs aligned to Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0. Associations were tested with MLM, BLINK and FarmCPU \citep{Slaten2020HAPPI}, with three genotype principal components as covariates (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_pca_scree}). For each model, Benjamini--Hochberg FDR was computed over the full scan and SNPs with FDR $\leq 0.05$ were declared significant. Candidate genes were B73 RefGen\_v5 genes within 25~kb of a significant SNP. Functional interpretation used maize gene annotations, MaizeGDB, Phytozome, and Arabidopsis homology resources.

To test whether soil-N associations reflected geography or climate, we ran the same pipeline on latitude, longitude, elevation, soil pH, CEC and aridity (Ref). The overlap between the significant SNP sets of each pair of variables was compared with its chance expectation, $n_1 n_2 / M$ ($M = 495{,}755$), using a one-sided hypergeometric test. In a second, model-free test, we computed the Pearson correlation between allele dosage (0, 1, 2; missing genotypes set to the SNP mean) and each site variable across the 3,107 accessions for every SNP, and expressed each SNP's $|r|$ as a genome-wide rank. A soil-N SNP was called linked to the environment if it ranked in the top 1\% for any non-N variable, not linked if it ranked in the top 5\% for soil N and not in the top 1\% for any other variable, and inconclusive if it ranked outside the top 5\% for soil N. The share of soil-N SNPs in the top 1\% for at least one geographic or environmental variable was compared, by binomial test, with the whole genome and with SNPs in the top 1\% for correlation with soil N.

\paragraph{Metabolite GWAS}

% \rra{several missing pieces here. Make sure numbers match results. Here you say 110 FAA + 77 PBAA but in results you mention 78 amino acid derived results}
Metabolite GWAS was conducted in the Goodman--Buckler Association Panel \citep{FlintGarcia2005Goodman} using 4,177,796 SNPs from the maize HapMap 3.2.1 genotypes of the 282-line panel, which are positioned on the B73 AGPv4 assembly. The dataset included 78 amino acid-related phenotypes: absolute and relative free amino acid traits, amino acid family composites, biochemical ratios, and the proxy traits \textit{Total\_N} and \textit{Total\_PBAA}. For each phenotype, best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs) were estimated before association analysis. GWAS used the HAPPI pipeline, which integrates phenotype preprocessing and association mapping and implements the GAPIT MLM, MLMM, BLINK, and FarmCPU models \citep{Slaten2020HAPPI}. Significance was declared at a 5\% Benjamini--Hochberg FDR, applied separately to each phenotype and model; MLMM gave no significant SNPs, so results come from MLM, BLINK and FarmCPU. Because the genotypes are on AGPv4, SNP positions were lifted to Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0 with the B73 RefGen\_v4-to-NAM-5.0 chain file, using same-chromosome forward-strand alignments and taking the highest-scoring alignment where several overlapped; 21 of the 2,461 significant SNPs fell in alignment gaps and were placed at the nearest aligned base. Candidate genes were B73 RefGen\_v5 genes within 25 kb upstream or downstream of each significant SNP, and significant SNPs within 25 kb of each other, across phenotypes, were merged into loci. A candidate gene was called trait-matched when its annotation (gene name and PANTHER family) placed it in the metabolism of the amino acid the phenotype reports on (the numerator amino acid, both amino acids of an amino acid ratio, or the members of a family composite) or of another amino acid in the same biosynthetic family, in amino acid, ammonium or nitrate transport, or in primary nitrogen assimilation (glutamine synthetase, glutamate synthase). This is a descriptive annotation, not an enrichment test.

To generate the \textit{Total\_N} proxy trait, amino acid measurements were converted to amino acid-derived nitrogen values by weighting each amino acid by its nitrogen content. For an amino acid $X$, the nitrogen mass fraction was calculated as

\[
\%N_X =
\left(
\frac{n_{N,X}\times M_N}{M_X}
\right)\times 100
\]

where $M_X$ is the molar mass of amino acid $X$, $M_N$ is the molar mass of nitrogen, and $n_{N,X}$ is the number of nitrogen atoms in amino acid $X$. amino acid-derived nitrogen for each amino acid was then calculated as

\[
N_X\;(\mu g/mg) =
\left(
\frac{n_{N,X}\times M_N}{M_X}
\right)
\times AA_X\;(\mu g/mg)
\]

and summed across amino acids to generate total proxy nitrogen values prior to BLUP estimation and GWAS.

\subsubsection{Quantitative Trait Loci Mapping}
We began with 2490 markers in total and following filtering of failed, monomorphic, and heterozygous markers 611 were used for the analysis. Markers were re-coded to be compatible with the r/qtl \citep{Broman2003} package used for mapping. Each allele was re-coded to AA to correspond with the allele associated with B64, BB for CML14, and AB if heterozygous. Best Linear Unbiased Predictions of absolute free aspartate content were calculated and used to construct the genetic map. To ensure quality of the genetic map, markers with more than 10\% inconclusive genotyping results were excluded and segregation ratios were checked to identify and remedy potential switched alleles. Linkage groups were formed by calculating max recombination fraction at 0.35 and minimum LOD at 15. Ultimately, one linkage group required manual separation and subsequent re-calculation of recombination fraction and LOD to produce an accurate genetic map to reflect the 10 chromosomes of maize. QTL scans were performed and significance thresholds were established at a=0.05 using 1000 permutations and Haley-Knott regression.

\subsection{STRING network analysis of candidate genes}

Protein association networks for candidate genes from all our analysis were constructed using STRING v12.0 (\url{https://string-db.org/}; \citealp{Szklarczyk2023STRING}). Candidate genes from our integrative analyses were queried against the \textit{Zea mays} network, and functional associations were retrieved from the STRING web interface using v4 gene version. We applied a stringent confidence cutoff, retaining only interactions with a high confidence score of $\geq 0.900$. 


\subsubsection{Publicly Available Tools and Datasets}
Genes within the confidence interval of the shared QTL were identified using B73 coordinates and the "Find gene models by position" tool in MaizeGDB \citep{MaizeGDB} and their annotations were sourced from Maize Mine \citep{maizemine}. Expression data for these genes were retrieved from qTeller \citep{qteller}, which provides RNA-seq-based transcript abundance across maize kernel tissues and developmental timepoints. Predicted protein-protein interactions among genes in the QTL region were obtained using STRING \citep{STRING}, which integrates known and predicted associations from multiple sources including text mining, experiments, co-occurrence, and databases.
